# Discipline when nobody is asking

> Nobody asks you to make the bed. That's why it's the first thing you decide to do.

In the last chapter I talked about fourteen years of service and three different institutions. In this one I talk about what those years left in me, and what I can carry over into civilian life. Some things transfer well. Others stayed behind, and that's no bad thing.

## What you learn about doing as you're told

When you're in an organisation with clear rules, discipline comes from outside. There's a timetable, there's a chain of command, there's a standard that somebody checks. You learn to wake up without an internal alarm, to finish a task even when you don't feel like it, and to trust that the whole works better than you do on your own.

That's valuable. But it has a limit that almost nobody explains to you: discipline that comes from outside disappears once the door shuts behind you. In civilian life, nobody checks whether you drank water, whether you paid the electricity bill or whether you did the task you left undone last week. If discipline doesn't start coming from inside, it disappears.

## Discipline isn't being hard

There's a very common wrong idea: discipline means putting up with everything without complaining, getting up early, ignoring tiredness. It doesn't. That's stubbornness, and stubbornness runs out fast.

The discipline that matters is closer to that of a good cook. The dish comes from order, not force: the ingredients measured, the worktop clean, the timing respected. When the order is there, you hardly need force at all.

## Routine is the foundation

In the last chapter I talked about foundations. Here the foundation is called routine. A well-designed routine doesn't trap you. It frees you, because it takes decisions out of your head that you don't need to make every morning.

Look at your weekday. How many decisions do you make before nine in the morning? What to eat, what time to leave, what to do first, whether you answer your phone. Every decision costs energy. A fixed routine turns those decisions into automatic habits, and the energy left over goes to what matters.

## The minimum daily standard

The idea I use most from all this is the minimum standard. Forget the ideal day. The minimum acceptable day is the one you do even on your worst days. For example: three things you always do, whatever the circumstances.

A minimum standard has three features. It's small enough not to be negotiable. It's visible, so you know whether you've done it. And it doesn't depend on motivation: you do it when you're feeling good and when you're feeling bad.

:::destaque
Motivation comes and goes. The minimum standard stays. Build discipline on the standard, not on the mood of the day.
:::

## The checklist nobody asks you for

In the organisations where I worked, the checklist was a safety tool. It wasn't paperwork: it was there so you wouldn't forget what's essential when you're tired or under pressure. In civilian life the checklist is still useful, and almost nobody uses one.

A personal checklist can have five lines: this month's bills checked, the week planned, the workout or walk done, the important conversation booked, ten minutes of tidying up. It doesn't have to look nice. It has to be used.

:::exercicio Your seven-day minimum standard
1. Write down the three things that, done every day, would change the direction of your week. Be concrete: "walk for 20 minutes" is better than "be healthier".
2. For each one, write the time you're going to do it.
3. Choose how you'll record it: a sheet of paper, an app or a calendar on the wall. There's no wrong way.
4. For seven days, mark each thing as done or not done. Don't explain anything, just tick.
5. On day eight, count how many you ticked. Don't judge the number. Adjust the difficulty.

:::

## Making the bed for the day

There's a phrase that has become almost a cliché, but it's still true: whoever makes their bed in the morning has already won the first small battle of the day. Not because the bed is important. Because it proves you can finish a task without anyone asking you to.

For me, making the bed is the metaphor for civilian discipline. It's a small thing, nobody sees it, but it sets the tone for the rest. When you don't make it, the day starts with a feeling of something left unfinished. When you do, the day starts with a small win that doesn't depend on anyone else.

:::historia
Illustrative example. Marta is 34, works part-time in an office in Lisboa and feels the week slipping through her fingers. She didn't have a big goal. She had a small habit: on waking, she tidied her bedroom in five minutes and opened her task notebook before she touched her phone. After a month, she hadn't changed jobs. She was sleeping better and felt that things were starting to move.
:::

:::alerta
A routine that's too rigid breaks in the first difficult week. If your plan demands perfection, it isn't discipline, it's a punishment with a timetable. If you miss a day, don't start again from zero: come back the next day, to the minimum standard.
:::

### When discipline becomes an excuse

There's a dark side to this conversation. Some people use discipline to avoid looking at whatever bothers them: they fill their calendar, do a thousand tasks and never touch what really matters. Being busy isn't the same as moving forward. Always ask: does this task bring me closer to what I want, or does it just keep me busy?

## When routine meets tiredness

Every routine is going to be tested. There'll be a week when work gets heavy, someone falls ill, the car bill arrives out of nowhere and sleep goes missing. That week, the routine you designed will feel like something that isn't for you. That's when you find out whether the discipline was real.

There are three responses that work better than giving up.

The first is to reduce, not abandon. If your minimum standard is three things, you do one. The second is to change the time, not the task. A walk at the end of the day instead of in the morning is still a walk. The third is to record the failure without commentary. Write "didn't do it" and carry on. The dangerous mistake isn't missing one day. It's missing one day and then missing a whole week, because the first miss turned into a story about you.

:::alerta
Be careful with guilt as a motor. Guilt helps you start, but it can't sustain the upkeep. A routine powered by guilt always ends up being dropped abruptly.
:::

## Discipline as respect

I like to think of discipline as a form of respect for the person you'll be tomorrow. When you keep your minimum standard today, you're giving that person something: a day when they don't start from zero. When you fail, tomorrow's you inherits the debt. This is a matter of logistics, not morality.

:::nota
On bad days, I do the minimum version.
Ten press-ups instead of a workout.
Two pages instead of a chapter.
What counts is not breaking the chain.
:::

## In summary

- Discipline that comes from outside disappears when the rules do. You have to build it from the inside.
- Discipline is order, not force.
- The minimum standard is what you do on bad days. That's what matters.
- A five-line personal checklist is worth more than a twenty-page plan you never open.

# The inventory

> Before you demolish, measure. Only what you know well can be taken apart properly.

In the last chapter I did the sums on time. Now I'm going to ask you for something more laborious and more honest: an inventory of your whole life. Not just work, not just money. Everything you have that, one way or another, is weighing on you.

An inventory sounds like paperwork. In practice it's the most personal thing you'll do in this book. It makes you look at what you have without making it prettier or more dramatic than it is.

## Why I start with the inventory

When a life starts to crumble, the temptation is simple: make a list of what bothers you and start throwing things out. That list is almost always wrong, because it looks at what weighs you down and not at what holds you up. Whoever follows it to the letter risks throwing out the very things that were holding the rest together.

The rule is this: before you decide what to demolish, you need to know what exists, what can bear weight and what is giving way. An inventory gives you that view. Without it, any decision is a guess made with good intentions.

## The four columns

I divide life into four columns. They aren't the only possible ones, but they gave me the most clarity, and they're the ones I'll use for the rest of the book:

1. **Money**: what comes in, what goes out, what you owe, what you've put aside.
2. **Energy**: what recharges you and what drains you, in body and mind.
3. **Time**: where the hours go, what's yours and what belongs to others.
4. **Relationships**: who adds to you, who pulls you down, who depends on you and who you depend on.

Each column has its own questions. Don't answer them all at once. Go through them over two or three days, calmly.

### The money column

- How much comes in each month, net? And how much goes out, really, including what you pay once a year?
- Which expenses can't you name off the top of your head?
- Do you have any debts? Which is the most expensive, as an annual percentage?
- Do you have any money put aside, and how long would it last if your income stopped?

### The energy column

- At what time of day do you feel most alive? And when do you just want to go to bed?
- Which activity leaves you feeling better afterwards, even if it takes effort?
- What drains your energy without giving you anything back?
- Are you sleeping enough for the life you have? If not, what's stopping you?

### The time column

- How many hours a week do you spend on things you chose consciously?
- How many do you spend on things that happened out of habit?
- Which task do you always put off, and why?

### The relationships column

- Who is the person you talk to when the news is bad?
- Who leaves you feeling worse after a conversation, even when there's no argument?
- Who do you depend on for specific tasks, and who depends on you?
- Which relationship are you keeping up out of obligation rather than choice?

:::cartao Keep · Repair · Demolish
- **Keep**: it works and gives me more than it takes.
- **Repair**: it does its job, but it's failing at one point I can fix.
- **Demolish**: it takes more than it gives and no reasonable repair will do.
:::

## Keep, repair or demolish

When you finish the inventory, each item falls into one of three categories. This is the part that gives the chapter its name.

| Category | What it means | Illustrative example |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep** | It works and bears weight. You leave it alone. | An emergency fund, even a small one, that already exists. |
| **Repair** | It has value, but it's giving way. It needs attention, not replacing. | A sleep habit that keeps drifting to the wrong hours. |
| **Demolish** | It's consuming more than it gives, and there's no reasonable repair. | A financial commitment that only keeps growing, or a relationship pattern that wears you out. |

The difficulty is almost always in the third column. It's easy to repair. It's hard to admit that something we chose for years no longer serves us. So before you mark anything as "demolish", ask yourself: is there a version of this I could repair first?

:::destaque
Demolition is the last option, not the first. Most things that call for demolition actually need serious repair and time.
:::

:::nota
Things I demolished first:
• Subscriptions I never even opened
• An hour of social media at night
• The "I'll look at that later" with the bills
:::

## Demolition isn't an attack

When I talk about demolishing, I'm not suggesting you drop everything overnight. A well-done demolition is controlled: you know what comes out, in what order, and what stays in place. A builder doesn't knock down a load-bearing wall without putting a prop in first.

In practice, demolishing might mean asking a creditor for more time, cutting an expense back rather than stopping it altogether, or stepping away from an activity for a month to see how you feel. It doesn't have to be a rupture. It can be a detour.

:::alerta
Don't demolish relationships on impulse. A bad argument, a tiring week or a day of frustration aren't enough reason to cut ties. If a relationship is doing you harm, talk to the person first, or to someone you trust, and only then decide.
:::

:::historia
Illustrative example. Tiago had three credit cards, a friendship that always left him feeling bad, and a job that filled his Saturday mornings. In the inventory he realised the biggest problem wasn't one thing: it was the sum of all four columns, all giving way at the same time. He started by repairing the money (he renegotiated one card), then set a time limit on weekend work. The friendship stayed unresolved for longer, and that was the decision that took the longest.
:::

## The long exercise

:::exercicio The four-column inventory
Do this exercise over two or three days. Don't rush it in one afternoon.

**Step 1. Money.** Answer the four questions in the column. Write down numbers, even approximate ones. If you don't know, write "don't know" and mark it so you can find out by the end of the week.

**Step 2. Energy.** Answer the four questions. Add a line with your average hours of sleep over the last seven days.

**Step 3. Time.** Answer the three questions. Use the table from the previous chapter as your starting point.

**Step 4. Relationships.** Answer the four questions. Write down the names of the people you come across every week and, next to each name, a sign: + (adds), = (neutral) or − (drains).

**Step 5. Classification.** For each item you wrote in the previous steps, choose a category: keep, repair or demolish. You can't leave any without a category.

**Step 6. First repair.** Choose the item marked "repair" that's easiest to deal with this week. Write what you're going to do, on which day and at what time.

**Step 7. First controlled demolition.** If you have any item marked "demolish", write the smallest first step to take it apart without collapse. Don't carry it out yet. Just write it down.

## In summary

- An inventory shows what exists before you decide what to change.
- The four columns are money, energy, time and relationships. They always move together.
- Most things need repair before they need demolition.
- A good demolition is controlled, with props, and never done on impulse.

## Your mission this week

Complete the four-column inventory and carry out only the first step of the chosen repair. Don't do anything more. The point is to see that you can move one thing without the rest falling apart.

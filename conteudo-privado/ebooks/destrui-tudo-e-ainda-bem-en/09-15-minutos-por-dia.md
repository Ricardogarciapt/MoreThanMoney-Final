# Fifteen minutes a day

> A short routine that's always the same is worth more than a long session full of improvisation.

When people talk about markets, the image that comes to mind is someone glued to the screen all day. That image is almost always the problem. The more time you spend staring at the price, the more decisions you make on impulse, and the harder it is to tell a plan from a hunch.

That's why the routine I suggest in this book is short. Fifteen minutes, in the morning or before a market window you chose in advance. It isn't a guarantee of anything. It's a way of limiting the time in which emotion can take charge.

:::destaque
Limited time doesn't make anyone smarter. It makes them less vulnerable to impulse, which is precisely the most common enemy of anyone starting out.
:::

## The structure of the 15 minutes

I divide the routine into three blocks. Each one has a clear purpose, and none of them is "finding the trade".

| Block | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Context | 5 min | Read today's news, the economic calendar and the state of your plan |
| 2. Checklist | 5 min | Confirm the pre-trade conditions, one by one |
| 3. Record | 5 min | Write in your journal what you decided and why, before any result |

If there's no valid condition on the checklist, block 2 ends with a sentence: "I'm not trading today." That's a trading decision as valid as any other, and often the best one.

## My routine, exactly as I do it

Before I ask you for a routine, let me show you mine. It has two parts, and the first has nothing to do with charts.

**Before trading: mind and body first**

1. **Mindset.** A few minutes to get my head right before I look at any price. I remind myself of the plan and the rules, not the money.
2. **Exercise.** Move the body before touching the account. A still body makes an impatient mind.
3. **Check how I am.** I ask myself honestly: did I sleep well? Am I calm? Am I carrying something from yesterday or from home? If the answer worries me, I don't trade today.
4. **Know where I'm going.** I reread my goals and the week I planned. Trading is one piece of the plan, not the whole plan.

**During trading: the Scanner Access checklist**

Only then do I open the scanners area and follow the same checklist as always, in this order:

| Block | What I confirm |
|---|---|
| Pre-trading routine | Today's news and economic calendar, correlations between markets and positions already open |
| Risk management | Risk per trade within my limit, total account exposure and a correctly calculated position size |
| Entry strategy | A valid setup from the plan, the signal confirmed and aligned with the trend |
| Exit strategy | A stop-loss for every position, targets and whether I'll exit in parts |
| Trade management | Follow the trade without impulsive changes, move the stop only by the rule and record the result and what I learned |

:::nota
The order is deliberate. The person first, then the market. On the days I skipped the first part, the second one paid for it.
:::

## Pre-trade checklist

The checklist is the most important part of the routine. It forces you to answer the questions that enthusiasm tries to skip. Print it, write it in a notebook or keep it on your phone, but always use the same one.

- [ ] I've read today's economic news and I know whether there are important releases in the next hour
- [ ] The asset I'm watching is the one in my plan, and not another that looked interesting
- [ ] The entry condition described in the plan is confirmed, not just "looks like it"
- [ ] I know where the stop-loss sits, in price, before I enter
- [ ] I've worked out the position size using the risk rule (chapter 8)
- [ ] The risk on this trade fits within the daily limit I wrote down
- [ ] I'm not trading to recover a previous loss
- [ ] I'm rested, sober and in no hurry

If any of these boxes is left unticked, you don't enter. It sounds rigid. In practice, it's what stops most poor-quality decisions.

:::alerta
The question "am I trading to recover a loss?" is the one that fails most often. Anyone who honestly answers "no" rarely feels pressure. Anyone who hesitates already has the answer.
:::

:::cartao Pre-trade checklist
- [ ] I know the trend on the bigger chart.
- [ ] The stop is set and fits within my 1%.
- [ ] There's no major news in the next 30 minutes.
- [ ] I'm calm and rested.
- [ ] If this trade loses, I'll be fine.

## The trading journal

The journal is where the routine stops being an idea and becomes data. Without it, you remember the trades that went well, you forget the others, and you end up telling yourself a story you like. With it, you can see the real pattern.

This is the model I use as a base. Each line is one decision, and the "why" column is written before the result.

| Date | Asset | Plan (condition) | Risk (€) | Size | Why (before) | Result (€) | Followed the plan? (yes/no) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | | | |
| | | | | | | | | |
| | | | | | | | | |

Notice that the last column is the most important. Often a trade with a positive result should be recorded as "no" in the plan column. Winning by chance and following the plan are different things, and the journal is the only place where that difference stays visible.

:::ecra Notes · Trading journal (example)
Date: Tuesday, 08:40
Pair: XAU/USD
Why I entered: uptrend, pullback to the average
Risk: 1%
Result: stop
How I felt: impatient, got in early
Lesson: wait for the confirmation candle
:::

:::curso Lesson 401 · Order types
There are two main ways of entering:

- **Market order:** you enter straight away, at whatever price is there. Quick, but sometimes a little worse than you saw.
- **Pending order (limit or stop):** you leave the order waiting for a price. You only get in if the price gets there.

For someone with fifteen minutes a day, pending orders are a blessing: you decide calmly, leave the order with a stop and a target, and get on with your life. The market comes to you, or it doesn't.

In the course: you build your first complete plan with a pending order, stop and target, on a demo account.
:::

## Stopping rules

Without stopping rules, the routine is just a nice timetable. The two I use most are these:

1. **Two losses and I close for the day.** After two losing trades in the same day, I don't open any more. No trading, no "just one more".
2. **Daily loss limit.** Before you start, write down the maximum loss you'll accept in a day (for example, 2% of capital). If you hit it, you shut the screen, even if the last trade looked obvious.

Illustrative example: on a $2,000 account, a 2% limit is $40. If the first two trades of the day lose, for example, $15 each, you've lost $30, still below the $40 limit. Even so, the two-loss rule says stop. It exists to protect you before you reach the limit in money.

:::exercicio Write your stopping rules
1. Maximum number of losses in the day: ______
2. Daily loss limit, as a percentage of capital: ______ %
3. Daily limit in money, worked out from step 2: ______
4. Time after which I don't open new trades: ______
5. A sign that I'm emotionally out of control (e.g. tight chest, checking my phone more): ____________________
6. What I do when that sign appears: ____________________
:::

:::nota
No clear signal today?
I close the platform.
Not trading is a decision too.
:::

## The Sunday review

Once a week, set aside thirty minutes on Sunday to read the journal. It isn't for judging. It's for understanding what the week taught you. The questions I always ask are these:

| Question | What it's for |
|---|---|
| How many decisions did I make following the plan? | Measures discipline, not result |
| How many did I make outside the plan, and at what time of day? | Shows your weak points |
| Was there any day when I respected the daily limit? | Checks whether the rule works |
| Is any mistake repeating itself? | Repeated mistakes are your real CV |
| What's the one thing I'll change next week? | Stops you changing everything at once |

The last question is the hardest. Changing one thing at a time is slower, but it's the only way to know what worked.

:::historia
Illustrative example. Someone does the Sunday review for four weeks and keeps noticing the same pattern: they get in after a loss, always with a bigger lot. In the fifth week, they decide that the only change is to shut the screen after the second loss. The change isn't spectacular. But it's the first time the account stops being a mystery.
:::

:::destaque
Educational content, not financial advice. A disciplined routine reduces common mistakes, but it doesn't guarantee results, and the risk of loss is real.
:::

## In summary

- Fifteen minutes a day, with three blocks: context, checklist and record.
- The checklist makes you answer the questions that enthusiasm skips.
- The journal records the decision before the result, and shows the real pattern.
- Two stopping rules, written before you start: the number of losses and the daily limit.
- The Sunday review is for learning, not judging. Change one thing at a time.

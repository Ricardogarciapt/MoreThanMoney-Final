# Money with structure

> Money without structure is like water without a glass: it runs wherever it likes.

We've arrived at money. Before I talk about pots, funds and investments, I want to make one thing clear: this chapter is educational. It isn't financial advice, and it doesn't replace a qualified professional when your situation calls for one. When it comes to investing, the risk of losing money is real, and you are the only person responsible for your decisions.

That said, let's get to what matters: most of the people I know don't have an income problem. They have a structure problem.

## Money has no memory

Money leaves without making a sound. A fifteen-euro subscription, a coffee every day, an online purchase halfway through the week, the renewal of an insurance policy nobody remembered. None of these things seems serious. Together, they add up to more than you'd imagine.

The problem is that money keeps no record of what happened. If you ask yourself at the end of the month "where did it go?", almost nobody can answer precisely. So the first rule is simple: money needs a destination before it reaches your account. Not afterwards.


## A budget in pots

The simplest way I know of giving money some structure is called pots. As soon as each income arrives, it's divided into different purposes. It can be a single account with sub-accounts, or several accounts. What matters is that each pot has a name and a limit.

An illustrative example, with a net income of €1,500 a month:

| Pot | Monthly amount | Percentage | What for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essentials | €900 | 60% | Rent, water, electricity, gas, internet, food, transport pass, getting around |
| Emergency fund | €150 | 10% | Serious surprises, until it reaches the target amount |
| Leisure | €150 | 10% | Dinners, cinema, outings, small purchases without guilt |
| Goals | €150 | 10% | Training, holidays, one planned expense for the year |
| Buffer | €150 | 10% | Everything that doesn't fit the others and always turns up |

Notice two things. The essentials are capped at 60%, and that forces you to review what is really essential. And the buffer pot is what stops you using the card when the washing machine breaks down on a Wednesday.

The numbers are an example. The proportion is the idea: no pot should be zero, and none should swallow the others.

:::alerta
Don't copy these percentages blindly. If rent alone eats up 60% of your income, the model has to be different. The budget should describe your life, not someone else's.
:::

## The emergency fund

An emergency fund is the money that exists so that a surprise doesn't turn into debt. It's commonly recommended that it covers between three and six months of essential expenses. It isn't a magic rule, but it's a sensible starting point.

Let's do the sum. If your essential expenses are €900 a month (the earlier example), the fund looks like this:

- three months: 900 × 3 = €2,700;
- six months: 900 × 6 = €5,400.

With €150 a month going into the pot, you reach €2,700 in about 18 months and €5,400 in about 36. It seems slow. But it's the pace at which most people build a foundation, and the alternative is a personal loan or the card, with interest that eats up whatever you'd have saved.

:::destaque
The emergency fund doesn't earn anything special and it isn't meant to. It's there so you can sleep soundly when something goes wrong.

Where should you keep the fund? In a separate account, ideally with no card attached and no easy access from your phone. If it's within sight, it's easier to dip into it for things that aren't emergencies.

## Expensive debts first

If you have debts, the first step isn't to invest or save seriously. It's to plug the leaky bucket. A debt with high interest is like a bucket with a hole in it: however much water you pour in, the level keeps dropping.

In most cases, the order that makes most sense is this: pay the minimum on every debt, and direct the rest to the one with the highest annual rate. Once that one is paid off, you move on to the next. This is sometimes called the avalanche method.

There's one exception to consider: if paying off the smallest debt first gives you the motivation to stick to the plan, that motivation is worth something. Choose the strategy you can keep up.

:::alerta
Be careful with credit that looks small. A low instalment, stretched over many months, can cost far more than the thing you bought. Before you sign, ask for the total amount payable and the TAEG (the annual effective global charge rate, Portugal's equivalent of the APR). Always compare.
:::

:::cartao The right order
1. Fixed bills paid.
2. Expensive debts being cleared.
3. Emergency fund growing.
4. Only then, invest. And only what you can afford to lose.
:::

:::curso Lessons 404 and 405 · Leverage and margin
Leverage lets you control a position bigger than the money you have in your account. Margin is the part of your money that stays locked up as collateral while the position is open.

Illustrative example: in Europe, for a retail trader, the maximum leverage on the main pairs is 1:30. A position of €10,000 in EUR/USD ties up about €333 of margin. That looks like very little. But the gain and the loss are calculated on the €10,000, not on the €333.

**Leverage doesn't give you more money. It gives you more exposure.** And more exposure, with money you need for the rent, is the fastest recipe I know for ruining a month.

In the course: you work out the margin by hand, on three different pairs, before you open your first position on a demo account.
:::

## Don't invest money you need

This is the rule I most like to repeat: don't invest money you're going to need in the coming years. Investing means accepting fluctuations, and those fluctuations can be strong. If you need the money during a bad phase of the market, you'll sell at a loss.

So the order is this: first structure (pots), then the emergency fund, then expensive debts, and only then, if it makes sense for your situation, investing with the money that's left over and that you don't need.

## Compound interest: the example that isn't a promise

Compound interest is the reason time matters so much with money. Interest is earned on the capital and on the interest that came before. After many years, the curve stops being a line and becomes a ramp.

I'll use an illustrative, hypothetical example. It isn't a forecast, it isn't a promise, and there is no guarantee of any return.

Suppose you set aside €100 a month for 20 years:

- with no return at all (0% a year): 100 × 240 months = €24,000 paid in by you;
- with a hypothetical rate of 5% a year, compounded monthly: about €41,100.

The difference, about €17,100, is interest. But notice what the sum doesn't show: the figure only exists if the money grows consistently for twenty years, without you withdrawing it, without the market falling at the worst moment and without the rate being different from the one you imagined. Any of these things changes the result, and it can change it for the worse.

:::alerta
Compound interest works for those who save and against those who owe. The same maths that makes your fund grow makes a credit card debt grow. Before you think about returns, deal with the expensive debts.
:::

## A place for money, day to day

You don't need a sophisticated spreadsheet. You need three things: a place where you record what comes in and what goes out, a fixed date to do it (for example, the last working day of the month) and a simple rule for any surplus. If there's money left over in the buffer pot, it goes to the fund. If the fund is complete, it goes to the most expensive debt. Only after that do you think about everything else.

Don't forget the annual charges. IRS (Portugal's income tax return), insurance, renewing documents, holidays. A pot for whatever turns up once a year stops these expenses catching you by surprise.

:::nota
Payday:
1st, the pots.
2nd, automatic saving.
3rd, everything else lives on what's left.
:::

## In summary

- Money needs a destination before it reaches your account.
- Pots give each purpose a name and a limit.
- The emergency fund, three to six months of essentials, is the first safety base.
- Expensive debts first. Don't invest money you're going to need.
- Compound interest is a powerful tool, but no example is a promise. The risk of loss exists.

## Your mission this week

Create your pots on paper or in a spreadsheet, with a name and a monthly amount for each. Work out your target fund of three months of essentials and write down the date you want to reach it. Don't buy anything new with the money in the buffer pot until you've done this.

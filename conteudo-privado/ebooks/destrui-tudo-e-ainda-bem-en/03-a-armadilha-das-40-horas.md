# The 40-hour trap

> A hundred and sixty-eight hours a week. Nobody sells them to you, but everyone spends them.

When I say "40 hours", almost everyone thinks of the contract. It's the number written on the paper, the one that appears on the payslip, the one the boss mentions at the interview. But a week doesn't have forty hours. It has a hundred and sixty-eight. And the way you divide them up is often the real reason you feel short of both time and money.

This chapter is about sums. Don't be afraid of them. They aren't hard, and it was by doing them that I understood what was happening to me.

## The sum of 168 hours

A week has seven days, and each day has twenty-four hours. Multiply and you get 168. Now ask yourself where each of them goes. Not where you should be spending them, but where they actually go.

Take a normal week and fill in the table with round numbers. The figures below are an illustrative example, to show you the logic. Replace them with yours.

| Activity | Hours per day | Days | Hours per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep | 7 | 7 | 49 |
| Work (contract) | 8 | 5 | 40 |
| Commuting | 3 | 5 | 15 |
| **Total** | | | **104** |
| Everything else (meals, hygiene, family, care, leisure, chores) | | | **64** |

Look at what the table shows. The 104 hours are already taken before you've started living. And what's left, 64 hours, has to cover everything else. Cooking, dealing with paperwork at the Serviço de Finanças (the Portuguese tax office), taking someone to the doctor, talking to the people you love. If your week looks like this, it isn't a lack of organisation. It's a lack of space.

## Work isn't just what's in the contract

The most misleading phrase in working life is "it's only the hours". The hours are the visible part. Underneath there's a layer almost nobody counts: the travel time, the preparation, the tiredness that stays behind, the dinner time that's no longer dinner time.

If you live in one place and work in another, the transport pass or the fuel gets paid for in money, but the time gets paid for in life. Three hours a day of commuting is fifteen hours a week. That's almost two full working days that don't show up on any payslip.

## The invisible cost of time

There's a mistake I made and I see it repeated: thinking that time is an endless resource, as long as the money lasts until the end of the month. It isn't. Money can be earned back. The time you spent on the road doesn't come back.

Think about it this way: if someone offered to swap three hours of your day for extra money, would you accept? Probably yes, if the amount was worth it. But almost nobody does that sum once the swap is already done, and the three hours simply vanish without you having chosen to give them up.

:::destaque
The salary on your payslip isn't your real hourly rate. Your real rate is what you earn per hour of life handed over, including everything that surrounds the work.
:::

## Your real hourly wage

This is the most useful sum in the chapter. It helps you see whether what you earn is worth the time you give to it.

:::exercicio What you really earn per hour
The figures below are an illustrative example. Use your own.

1. Write your monthly net income. Example: €1,500.
2. Write the contracted working hours per week. Example: 40 h.
3. Add up your daily commuting time and multiply by five working days. Example: 3 h × 5 = 15 h.
4. Add the two weekly figures. Example: 40 + 15 = 55 h.
5. Convert to a month: multiply by 52 and divide by 12. Example: 55 × 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 238 h a month.
6. Divide your income by the monthly hours. Example: €1,500 ÷ 238 ≈ €6.30 an hour.
7. Do the same sum with only the 40 contracted hours: 40 × 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 173 h a month. €1,500 ÷ 173 ≈ €8.67 an hour.

The gap between the two values is the invisible cost of your commute. In the example, it's around two euros and thirty cents an hour that never appears on the payslip.

Net income: €______________

Contracted hours per week: ______ h

Commuting per week: ______ h

Total monthly hours (step 5): ______ h

Real hourly value (step 6): €______
:::

You don't need to conclude anything with this number today. You need to know it. Many important decisions (moving house, changing job, taking a training course, asking for a raise) become clearer once you know what your time is worth in practice.

:::alerta
Don't confuse this sum with a verdict on your job. A job with a low hourly value can be the right one for a phase of life, for family, health or learning reasons. The sum is there so you can decide with your eyes open, not so you can judge yourself.
:::

## Quality hours and leftover hours

Not all hours are worth the same. An hour of conversation with the family at dinner isn't the same quality as an hour of pointless screen time, and neither of them can be measured in money. So, as well as counting hours, it's worth separating three types:

- **paid hours** (the contract, the invoicing, what goes into your bank account);
- **maintenance hours** (hygiene, meals, shopping, paperwork, looking after others);
- **life hours** (proper rest, family, time with no goal, learning that doesn't get a grade).

When the table of 168 hours is done properly, you almost always find that the life hours are the first to disappear. Not because you cut them, but because nobody protects them. The maintenance hours grow to fill the space, and the life hours are left for later, which never comes.

:::exercicio Protecting three life hours
1. In your table, identify the block of life hours you have today, on average per week.
2. Choose three hours you'll protect the following week. Write the day and time of each one.
3. Put them in the calendar as appointments, with the same status as a meeting.
4. On Sunday, count how many were actually kept. You don't have to make the most of them. It's enough that you held on to them.

:::

:::curso Lesson 410 · Trading sessions
The currency market never sleeps, but it isn't always the same either. It has four sessions: Sydney, Tokyo, London and New York. In Lisboa time, in winter, it roughly looks like this:

- **Tokyo:** from midnight to 9am. Calmer. It's the so-called Asian window.
- **London:** from 8am to 5pm.
- **New York:** from 1pm to 10pm.
- **London and New York at the same time:** from 1pm to 5pm. This is when there's the most volume and the most movement.

The conclusion is simple and it changes how you look at your time: you don't need the whole day. You need to know when to look.

## What to do with the sum

Once you've done the sums, there are three questions that help you move forward without rushing:

- Which hour of the week can you give back to yourself without touching your job? It could be lunch, a Saturday morning, the end of a Wednesday afternoon.
- Is there any commute you could shorten, reorganise or split? One day a week working from home, if it's possible, already changes the sum.
- Which part of the other 64 hours is being eaten up by distractions you didn't choose? Screens, notifications, pointless meetings.

Don't rush. The 40-hour trap doesn't come undone in a week. It comes undone when you start treating time as the most expensive resource you have.

## In summary

- The week has 168 hours. The contract takes up a small part of them.
- Commuting time is unpaid work that doesn't show up on the payslip.
- Your real hourly wage is almost always lower than you imagine.
- You don't need to change everything. You just need to know where the hours are.

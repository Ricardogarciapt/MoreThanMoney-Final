# Buying for the first time

> Your first purchase is not there to make money. It is there to learn how everything works with the least possible damage.

There is a big difference between knowing what crypto is and buying crypto. The theory can be read over a weekend. The practice has buttons, fees, checks and small details that only make sense once real money is on the line. This chapter is a guide to the very basics: where to buy, how you sign up, which type of order to choose and how to get the first euro in without rushing.

## Where to buy, on a regulated platform

A platform where you buy and sell crypto is called an exchange, or crypto broker. Choose one that is regulated in the European Union, ideally authorised under MiCA, which I mentioned in chapter three. Look for that information on the site itself, in the terms and conditions and on the warnings page of the regulator in the country where the company operates.

Do not choose because of an advert, a welcome bonus or a friend who "already uses it". Choose on criteria you can check: regulation, transparent fees, customer support, account security and reputation over time.

## Registration and identity checks

To buy, you will have to sign up. The technical term is KYC, short for know your customer. The platform will ask for your name, your date of birth, an address and an identity document, such as your Cartão de Cidadão (Portuguese citizen card) or your passport. It may also ask for a selfie.

This is normal and it protects you, because it helps fight fraud and money laundering. Be wary, however, of platforms that ask for information that makes no sense for a purchase, or that ask for your bank password.

:::alerta
Never send your password, your two-step verification code or your recovery key to anyone, not even to a platform that contacts you first. A legitimate platform will never ask you for that by message.
:::

:::post @morethanmoney.pt

## Fees, and what you pay without noticing

Every platform has fees. Some charge a percentage of the value of the purchase. Others charge a spread, which is the gap between the buy price and the sell price. There are also deposit fees, withdrawal fees and network fees, when you transfer the crypto to another wallet.

It may look like a small detail, but it is not. Imagine you buy €100 of a coin with a total fee of 2%. You immediately have €98 of real value, before the market has moved at all. Before you buy, look for the fee page and do the sum.

## Market order or limit order

When you buy, the platform asks what type of order you want. There are two basic options.

- **Market order:** you buy at whatever price is available at that moment. It is quick, but you may pay a little more than you expected, especially when the market is busy.
- **Limit order:** you set the maximum price you are willing to pay. The order only executes if the market reaches that price. It gives you more control, but it may never execute.

For a beginner, a limit order usually gives you more control. It is not mandatory, and the choice depends on what you want to do.

:::destaque
On your first purchase, the most important thing is not the perfect price. It is knowing exactly what you are buying, how much you are paying in fees and where the crypto is going to be kept.
:::

:::curso Lesson 3.2 · The real cost of buying
The price you see is not the price you pay. Between the platform fee, the spread and the withdrawal fee, a small purchase can cost more than it looks.

- Always do the sum beforehand: how much goes in, how much arrives, how much gets lost on the way.
- On a small purchase, a fixed fee weighs far more.

In the course: you compare the cost of three ways of buying the same amount, with the numbers in front of you.
:::

## The first euro

Here is a simple route for a first purchase, with a small, controlled amount.

1. Choose an amount you could lose without suffering, for example €10.
2. Make the purchase with a market order, so you understand the flow.
3. Check the amount, the fee and the coin before you tap confirm.
4. After buying, wait a few hours and watch the movement without reacting to every swing.

This first step is not there to make money. It is there to get you familiar with the interface, the statements and the feeling of watching a balance move. That familiarity is worth more than any small gain.

## Illustrative example, the cost of a small purchase

**Illustrative example**, with round, made-up figures: you buy €50 of a coin, with a 1.5% fee. You pay €0.75 in fees and end up with €49.25 invested. If the price rises 10% the next day, your balance is worth about €54.18. If the price falls 10%, it is worth about €44.33. The fee has already put you behind at the start. These numbers are there only to show the logic, not as a forecast.

## A short story

:::historia
Illustrative case: Miguel, 41, a teacher in Lisbon, bought his first crypto one night, on impulse, after watching a video. He did not understand the fee, did not read the help page and used a market order in the middle of a big swing. The next day, his balance was below what he had paid. The lesson he took from it was simple: hurry is the worst adviser for a first purchase.
:::

## In short

- Choose a platform regulated in the European Union and check the information yourself.
- KYC is normal, but never share passwords or codes.
- Fees are a real cost that you should work out before buying.
- A market order is quick; a limit order gives you more control.
- Your first purchase should be small and serve to learn.

## To do today

1. Write down the name of the platform you are considering and check on its website whether it is regulated in the European Union.
2. Read the fee page and work out how much you would pay on a €10 purchase.
3. Write down the maximum you would invest the first time, and do not raise it afterwards.
4. If you do not have an account yet, do not sign up today. Go through the checklist first and decide tomorrow.

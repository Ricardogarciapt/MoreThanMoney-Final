# Your anti-scam shield

> A scammer does not need to be clever. He just needs you to be in a hurry.

Picture an ordinary Tuesday, at twenty to seven in the evening, with dinner still to make. Your phone buzzes. The message says: "We have detected a strange login to your account. Confirm the code you have just received within the next 15 minutes." You read it twice. Your heart speeds up and your hand is already reaching for the code. That is the moment this chapter wants you to recognise.

Crypto scams change their name and their face, but they keep repeating. The transactions are fast, almost always irreversible, and the vocabulary is confusing enough that nobody wants to look ignorant by asking. Those trying to deceive you know this better than we do. So it is worth knowing the patterns rather than trusting your instinct in the moment.

## The five patterns that keep coming back

**Fake support.** A message, call or email from someone claiming to be from the platform, the wallet or a help service. They say your account is locked or that there is a suspicious transaction, and they ask for codes, passwords or your seed phrase. Genuine support never asks for those.

**"Doubling" crypto.** You send €500 and they promise to return €1,000. It shows up in videos with faked public figures, in giveaways and in live streams. There is no legitimate way to double money.

**Phishing.** A website that looks almost identical to the real one, with one letter swapped or an extra hyphen in the address. When you enter your credentials, they go straight to the person deceiving you.

**Fake airdrops.** Free coins appear in a wallet you never asked for. When you try to "claim" them, the app asks for an authorisation that gives access to your money. If you did not ask for it, do not claim it.

**Romance scams.** Someone meets you on a dating app or on social media, builds a relationship over several weeks and then gently suggests you invest together in a "fantastic" platform. The money disappears, and so does the person. It is one of the most painful scams, because it plays on emotions.

:::alerta
If someone you met online suggests you invest, points you to a platform and asks you not to talk to anyone about it, you are looking at a scam. Stop. Speak to someone you trust before making any transfer.
:::

This is the typical look of a scam that starts with a message. It is not a real case, just an example so you can recognise the tone:

:::conversa WhatsApp · Unknown number
Wallet Support: Hello. We have detected an unauthorised access. To protect your balance, send us your recovery phrase now.
Wallet Support: You have 15 minutes, otherwise the account will be locked for good.
eu: I will go through the official site, which I have noted in my notebook. I am not sending anything to anyone.
:::

## The rule of hurry

Almost all of these scams share one thing: urgency. "You have 30 minutes." "The offer ends today." "The account will be closed." Hurry stops you from thinking, from checking and from talking to someone. A genuine transaction can wait for your time. If you feel the pressure rising, get up, drink a glass of water and come back to it in an hour.

:::destaque
No serious platform will ever make you feel urgent. When someone asks you to hurry, hurry is their weapon.
:::

## Read the address letter by letter

Phishing lives in the detail. The address you received by message may look right at first glance, and only when you read it carefully do you realise it is not. This is the kind of thing that slips past you at seven in the evening, with your phone in your hand:

:::ecra Example · suspicious address (illustrative)
Address received: www.exemplo-carteira-verificacao.net
Saved address: www.exemplo-carteira.pt
Asks for: recovery phrase
Status: fake ✗
:::

So never click on links that arrive by message. Type the address yourself or use the bookmark you already have saved.

## The ten-point checklist

Write this list on a piece of paper and stick it near your screen. Before any transaction, or before replying to a message, go through the points.

:::cartao Before you confirm
- Did I ask for this contact, or did it arrive unexpectedly?
- Is someone asking me for passwords, codes or the seed phrase?
- Is the address spelled correctly, letter by letter?
- Is there pressure, a threat or an impossible deadline?
- Does the promise involve free money or doubling values?
- Can I confirm the information through another official channel?
- Am I acting out of fear of missing out?
- Have I talked to a trusted person about this?
- Are the transaction's address, network and amount confirmed?
- If this goes wrong, can I live with the loss?
:::

If the answers to the first five questions are worrying, you do not go ahead. If the answers to the last five are uncertain, you do not go ahead either.

:::curso Lesson 5.4 · The ten-point checklist
No serious company will ask you for your seed phrase. No support team will write to you first. And anyone promising to double your money is not being serious.

In the course: you practise with real scam messages, with the names removed, and learn to recognise them in seconds.
:::

## Layers of security

Protection works in layers. No single measure solves everything, but together they make your money much harder to steal.

[[grafico:camadas-seguranca]]

Start with the base: a strong, unique password, two-step verification switched on and your phone kept up to date. Then separate: an account just for crypto, with a dedicated email address. For larger amounts, use a cold wallet. And at the top sits the habit of never reacting on impulse.

:::nota
Today: open your notebook and write down the official phone number and email of the platform where you have an account. If someone calls you, hang up and use those contacts.
:::

## A short story

:::historia
Illustrative case: Sofia, 52, a classroom assistant in Braga, received a call from someone claiming to be from her bank and her crypto platform at the same time. They asked her for a code that had just arrived on her phone. She hesitated, hung up and rang the official number she had saved. It was nobody from the platform. Hanging up and checking is what saved her.
:::

## In short

- The most common scams are fake support, doubling schemes, phishing, fake airdrops and romance scams.
- Urgency is almost always the warning sign.
- Never share passwords, codes or the seed phrase.
- Always confirm through an official channel that you chose yourself.
- Security works in layers, and calm is the last one.

## To do today

1. Open the list of saved sites in your browser and confirm, letter by letter, each address.
2. Switch on two-step verification for your main email account, if it is not already active.
3. Write by hand the official phone number and email of the platform where you have an account.
4. Copy the ten-point checklist onto paper and stick it near your screen.

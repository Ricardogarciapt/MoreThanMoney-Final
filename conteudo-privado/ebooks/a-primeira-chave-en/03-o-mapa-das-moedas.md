# The map of the coins

> There are thousands of coins. Learning to tell a tool from a fairground poster is half the battle.

When you start researching crypto, the first feeling is dizziness. There are animal names, English words that mean nothing, three-letter tickers and coins that seem to have been invented one afternoon. I am going to try to put some order into this. I am not going to give you a shopping list. I am going to give you a map, so you can look at any coin and at least work out what kind it is.

## Bitcoin, the first and the simplest

Bitcoin is the original coin. It has a cap of 21 million units, a rule that no team can change by decision. It also has a mechanism called halving: every 210,000 blocks, which happens on average every four years, the reward paid to miners is cut in half. The creation of new coins therefore slows down over time.

The reward has followed this path: 50 units at the start, 25 in 2012, 12.5 in 2016, 6.25 in 2020 and 3.125 in 2024.

[[grafico:halving]]

The halving is a known, public rule, but that does not guarantee the price will go up. Some people see it as a reason to buy. Others see it as just one more data point among many. The rule is clear; reading the market is subjective, and everyone does it their own way.

## Ethereum, the platform that runs programs

Ethereum launched in 2015 and it is different. It is not just a coin. It is a platform where smart contracts run, which are programs that carry out rules automatically when certain conditions are met. Many decentralised finance applications and digital art projects live here. Since September 2022 it has used proof-of-stake, as we saw in the previous chapter.

For a beginner, the most useful thing is to grasp the underlying difference: Bitcoin was designed mainly as digital money, and Ethereum as a platform where applications run. They have different goals, and different risks.

## Stablecoins, the dollar inside crypto

Stablecoins are coins that aim to hold a steady value, usually pegged to the US dollar. The best known are USDC and USDT. They are widely used to hold value without leaving the crypto ecosystem, or to move money quickly.

Be careful, because a stablecoin is not risk-free money. It depends on the issuer, on the reserve it says it holds and on trust that the reserve really exists. If the issuer runs into trouble, the coin can lose its peg to the dollar. For someone looking for stability it is a useful tool, but it means you have to read up on who issues it.

## Altcoins and memecoins, the rest of the map

Every coin that is not Bitcoin is called an altcoin. Among them are projects with serious technology and projects that exist mainly to attract attention. Memecoins are the extreme case: they are often born from a joke, an internet image or a post, and their value depends almost entirely on who comes in and who gets out. They can rise a lot and fast, and fall even faster. Treat them for what they are: bets with very high risk.

:::alerta
A coin with a lot of marketing, plenty of influencers saying it is "about to explode" and very little verifiable information is a warning sign, not an opportunity.
:::

## The seven questions for judging a project

Before you think about buying any coin that is not Bitcoin, ask these seven questions. They do not guarantee anything, but they make you think.

1. **What problem does it solve?** If you cannot explain it in two sentences, be suspicious.
2. **Who is behind it?** Is there a team with verifiable names, or is everything anonymous?
3. **Is there public technical documentation?** A clear whitepaper, open-source code, dates and milestones.
4. **Who holds most of the coins?** If a few addresses control most of the supply, the risk of manipulation is high.
5. **How does the coin gain value?** If the answer is "just because it will go up", that is not an answer.
6. **Are there independent audits or reviews?** Ask for proof, not promises.
7. **If the project disappeared tomorrow, what would be left?** This question is uncomfortable, and it is the most honest of all.

:::cartao The seven questions, in brief
- What problem does it solve?
- Who is behind it?
- Is there public documentation?
- Who holds the majority?
- Why does it have any value?
- Are there audits?
- What is left if it ends?
:::

:::destaque
A serious project answers the hard questions with facts. A scam answers them with urgency.
:::

## MiCA, the European rulebook

In the European Union, the MiCA regulation (the EU's rules for crypto-assets) has applied to crypto-asset service providers since 30 December 2024. This means platforms operating in Europe have to meet requirements on transparency, customer protection and governance. It does not solve everything, but it shows the market is being put in order. When you choose where to buy, always check whether the platform complies with this rule.

## In short

- Bitcoin has a cap of 21 million and a halving every 210,000 blocks.
- Ethereum has been a smart contract platform since 2015.
- Stablecoins depend on their issuer and reserve, and they are not free of risk.
- Altcoins and memecoins carry very high risk.
- The seven questions help you separate serious projects from bets dressed up with marketing.

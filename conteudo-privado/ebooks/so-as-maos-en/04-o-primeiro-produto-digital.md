# Your first digital product
> You don't need the perfect product. You need the first one that someone wants enough to write to you about.

There's a classic trap for beginners: spending months planning a huge course, with ten modules, bonuses, videos with good lighting and a flawless sales page, and never launching anything. When it finally launches, they don't know whether anyone needs it, because they never asked.

Your first digital product is there to teach you. It should be small, clear and made with care, but it doesn't have to be complete. In this chapter you'll see the most common types of product, how to validate them before you build them, and how to think about a ladder of value that gives you room to grow.

## What a digital product is

A digital product is content you deliver without stock, without shipping and without packaging. It can be a file to download, access to a private area, a set of videos or a template the person uses on their computer. You sell it once, or by subscription, and it can be delivered automatically.

That nature is a huge advantage: the cost of delivering one more copy is almost zero. But it also has a downside. Because there's nothing physical, all the value has to sit in how clearly you describe what you're offering.

## The most common types

The table below compares the most used formats. The timings are only rough orders of magnitude, because they depend heavily on your availability and how complex the topic is.

| Type | What it is for | Rough time to create | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| E-book | Going deeper into a topic with text and images | A few weeks | Balcony vegetable garden guide for beginners |
| Template | Saving time with a ready-to-use model | A few days | Content planning template for the month |
| Pocket guide or checklist | Solving one specific step | A few hours | Checklist for posting a story without mistakes |
| Planner | Organising a routine | A few days | Weekly planner for shift workers |
| Mini-course | Teaching a process in stages | A few weeks | Four short lessons on making simple invoices |

No format is better in the abstract. The best one is the format that solves your person's problem, within the time you have to build it.

## Validate before you build

Validating means confirming that someone wants something before you spend weeks making it. There are simple ways to do this:

1. **Ask ten people.** Don't ask "do you think you would buy it?", because everyone says yes. Ask: "When did you last try to sort this out? What did you do?"
2. **Create a waiting list.** A simple page or a form where people leave their email to say they want to hear when it's ready. If nobody signs up, you've learnt something.
3. **Sell the small version first.** If you already have a part ready, offer it at a low price with clear delivery. If you have nothing yet, be open about the delivery timeline.

## The ladder of value

A ladder of value is the sequence of products and touchpoints that takes someone from first contact to the most complete product. You don't have to build it all at once. But having the ladder drawn helps you see where your first product fits.

[[grafico:escada-valor]]

The rungs usually look like this:

- **The free lead magnet.** A short guide or checklist that answers one small question. It lets the person try your way of teaching.
- **The entry product.** A template or guide at a low price that solves one specific problem.
- **The core product.** A mini-course or a more in-depth e-book, for people who want to go further.
- **The follow-up.** A community, a mentoring programme or meetups, for people who want support.

A free lead magnet isn't a gift with no purpose. It collects the email of whoever asks for it, with consent, and gives you the chance to explain the next step.

## How to choose the first one

To choose your first product, answer four questions honestly:

| Question | If the answer is no |
|---|---|
| Do I know this well enough to teach it? | Study more or pick another topic. |
| Are there people looking for this solution? | Validate with questions before you go ahead. |
| Can I deliver in about thirty days? | Narrow the scope until you can. |
| Does it fit into my week without burning me out? | Simplify the format. |

:::historia
**Illustrative case: Rui.** Rui is a fictional character. He spent two months planning a complete photography course. When he asked a few people what was holding them back, he heard something different: they wanted to photograph the products in their online shops with their phones and didn't know where to start. Rui wrote a twelve-page guide on exactly that. It wasn't the course he'd imagined, but it was the first product people asked him for back, with questions and doubts. That's how he worked out what to build next.

## In summary

- Your first product should be small, clear and validated. It doesn't need to be complete.
- The most common formats are the e-book, the template, the pocket guide, the planner and the mini-course.
- Validate with open questions, a waiting list or a small version.
- The ladder of value shows you where your first product fits in the person's path.
- I'm not promising the first product will bring in money by the end of the month. Results depend on each person's effort.

## To do today

1. Write three product ideas in your phone's notes, each with the problem it solves in one sentence.
2. Send the questions from the validation card to three people today, by message. You don't need ten to start.
3. Create a free form with name, email and one sentence saying what you're preparing. Include a consent line so you can get in touch.

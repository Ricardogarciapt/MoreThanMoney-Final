# The client who comes looking for you
> You don't sell to an audience. You sell to one person who, tonight, has a specific problem.

When someone starts planning a digital product, they tend to think of "women aged 25 to 40" or "entrepreneurs". These are useful categories for a presentation, but they don't help you write a sentence someone will read on their phone, on the metro. To sell, you need a real person, with a real pain and real words.

In this chapter we're going to build that person, work out what bothers them and learn to use their words, not yours.

## Who the person on the other side is

Think of Sofia (illustrative example). She's thirty-two, works in an office in Lisbon, pays rent and has a schedule that starts at nine and ends when it ends. She likes cooking at the weekend, but never gets round to organising the recipes she keeps on her phone. She wants to start doing something of her own, but she's worried she won't have the time or the knack.

The detail is what matters. It isn't enough to say that Sofia "wants to earn some extra money". You need to know what she does in the evening, what tires her out, what makes her hesitate and where her free time goes. That's what lets you write something that feels as if it was written for her.

The more concrete the person you imagine, the easier it is to write for a real person, rather than for an average that interests nobody.
:::

## Pain, desire and language

Every client has three layers you need to map:

- **The pain:** what bothers them today. Tiredness, lack of time, fear of failing, the feeling of always being behind.
- **The desire:** what they would like to have or feel. A project that's theirs, a lighter routine, the sense of moving forward.
- **The language:** the exact words they use to talk about this. She doesn't say "I need to optimise my time". She says "I can't leave work and still do something for myself".

The third layer is the most important and the most ignored. When you write using the person's own words, they recognise themselves. When you write in jargon, they feel you're talking about something else.

| Pain | How the person says it | Desire | How the person says it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lack of time | "I don't have the energy for this at the end of the day." | Having a project of her own | "I wanted to do something that was mine." |
| Fear of failing | "What if this doesn't work and I waste my time?" | Feeling she's moving forward | "I wanted to see some progress, however small." |
| Lack of knack | "I'm not good with technology." | Being able to | "I wanted to understand how it's done, without complications." |

## Where your client's language lives

Real language can't be invented. Look for it where people are already talking about the problem you solve:

- Comments on other people's posts that cover topics similar to yours.
- Questions people ask in groups, forums or Q&A sections.
- Reviews of similar products, especially the critical ones. Complaints are gold dust, because they show what was missing.
- Messages you've already received from friends and strangers about the topic.

Keep the phrases that come up most often in a list. You'll use them as titles for posts, as hooks for stories and as the basis for your DM conversations.

:::cartao Phrases to copy into the list
- "I don't have the energy for this at the end of the day."
- "I wanted to do something that was mine."
- "I'm not good with technology."
:::

:::alerta
Don't copy other people's texts or phrases as they are. Use the pattern of the language (the type of word, the doubt, the emotion) and write your own version. Never quote someone else's comment without their permission and without anonymising it.
:::

## The empathy map

The empathy map is a sheet divided into four quadrants, which helps you put the person on paper. The quadrants are: what they think and feel, what they see, what they hear and what they say and do. At the bottom of the map you bring together the pain and the gain, that is, what they want to get rid of and what they want to achieve.

[[grafico:mapa-cliente]]

You don't need a sophisticated tool. A sheet of A4 folded in four will do. The value is in the act of writing, because it forces you to think about things you would otherwise take for granted.

:::exercicio My client's empathy map
1. Give the person a name and an age. Write one sentence about their job and their routine.
2. **Thinks and feels:** what goes through her head when she wakes up? And on Sunday night?
3. **Sees:** what appears in her feed? What kind of content makes her stop, and what kind makes her scroll past?
4. **Hears:** what do friends, family and colleagues tell her? What advice has she received that didn't work?
5. **Says and does:** what does she say out loud, in private conversations, in groups? What does she actually do when nobody is watching?
6. **Pain and gain:** what's the biggest pain she wants to leave behind? What's the biggest gain she imagines?

:::

## The offer comes from the pain, not from your taste

A very common mistake is to create a product from what we're passionate about. You love photography, so you make a "photography course". The problem is that people don't buy photography. They buy the solution to a pain.

Compare:

- "Photography course" → a generic desire, with no urgency.
- "Guide to ten angles for photographing food at home with your phone, without studio lights" → a specific pain, concrete language, an outcome that's easy to picture.

The second version is shorter, more honest and easier to sell, because the person recognises themselves straight away.

:::conversa WhatsApp · Sofia (example)
Sofia: I saw your post about the recipes. Is this for people who've never organised anything like that?
eu: It's for people who have recipes saved and have never put them together. If that's you, it could help.
Sofia: I think so. How long does it take?
eu: Ten minutes to get started. No rush. If it doesn't suit you, tell me and that's completely fine.
:::

:::alerta
Don't write for everyone. Someone who tries to please everybody ends up saying nothing to anybody. A well-defined client doesn't stop others from buying, but it gives your message a recipient.
:::

## How to speak to her with your hands

This map has a practical consequence for hands-based content. If Sofia is worried she won't have the knack, show your hands doing something simple, in real time, without over-the-top cuts and with the step-by-step easy to read. If she has no time, show the process in three minutes rather than twenty. The hands don't need to talk. They need to show that the problem has a solution and that it can be done with little time.

The charts and examples you'll see throughout the book always come back to one question: who is the person, and what is pulling them away from where they are?

## In summary

- Sell to one specific person, with a specific pain and a specific desire.
- Your client's words are worth more than yours. Collect them where they already exist.
- The empathy map makes you think about what the person sees, hears, says and does.
- An offer is born from a specific pain, not from your personal taste.

## To do today

1. Write the name, age and job of the person you want to help. One sentence is enough.
2. Fill in the six lines of the empathy map, on paper or in a note on your phone.
3. Open a group or forum where your topic is discussed and copy three real questions into your list of phrases.
4. Today, send a message to a trusted person who isn't family and ask: "What's the thing that costs you most about this topic?" Listen to the answer without correcting it.

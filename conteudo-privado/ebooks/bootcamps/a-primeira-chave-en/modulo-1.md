# Módulo 1 — Money, blockchain and Bitcoin (≈17 min)
**Objetivo:** understand what gives money its value, why Bitcoin was created and how a blockchain works, without jargon.  **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) explain why money depends on trust; 2) calculate the effect of inflation with a simple sum; 3) explain a blockchain as a shared notebook and say what Bitcoin was in 2008.

## Aula 1.1 — What money actually is (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "A fifty-euro note is a piece of paper. So why do you agree to trade a day's work for it?"

**[CENA 1]** Black screen. Text appears letter by letter: "Educational content. This is not financial advice. The risk of loss is real." Then Ruben in a medium shot, almost black background, gold details.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Hi, I'm Ruben Pereira, and this is the A Primeira Chave bootcamp. Before we start, there's a warning you must not skip. This bootcamp is educational. It is not financial advice. I won't tell you what to buy, or when. Crypto is a high-risk market: prices can fall a lot, and you can lose everything you put in. So the first rule of this house is this: never invest money you can't afford to lose. If you need it for next month's rent, that money is not for crypto. I've said it, and I'll say it again at the end of the module. If there's one thing from this bootcamp that stays with you, let it be this.

Now let's get to the beginning. What is money?

It sounds like a childish question, but almost nobody answers it well at first. Money is anything we accept as payment because we know others will accept it too. Take a fifty-euro note. On its own, it's paper with nice colours. It has value because the baker accepts it, because the landlord accepts it, because the Portuguese tax authority (Autoridade Tributária) accepts it. The value sits in people's trust, not in the paper.

Think about what happens when you receive a bank transfer. You don't see a safe opening. You see a number change on a screen, and you trust that this number stands for something real, because everyone around you acts as if it does. You're trusting a shared list of records that other people also accept. And it's this collective trust, not the material, that makes money work every day.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: a fisherman with a basket of horse mackerel at the door of a bakery. Two arrows cross between the two of them, and a question mark appears between the arrows.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before money, people swapped things. Imagine you're a fisherman in Peniche. You take a basket of horse mackerel to the bakery, because you need bread. But what if the baker doesn't want fish that day? Or what if the fish spoils before he wants it? Economists call this the lack of a double coincidence of wants: the person who has what you want doesn't always want what you have. Direct swapping works, but it's slow and depends a lot on luck.

**[CENA 3]** Animated timeline, from left to right: shells, salt, metal coins, a paper note and, at the end, a balance flashing on a phone screen. At each step the object disappears and the same word stays in the centre: "trust".
**NARRAÇÃO:** That's why, throughout history, people chose objects that almost everyone accepted. There were shells, salt, livestock, metals. Then came coins, and after them paper notes. Today, most money isn't even paper. It's a number on your bank's screen. You pay for a coffee with your phone, you send money through a payment app, and none of it passes from hand to hand. It's still money, because you still trust the system that recorded it.

**[CENA 4]** A castle with three pillars, gold on charcoal. The pillars have captions: "the baker accepts", "the landlord accepts", "the State accepts". One of the pillars starts to tremble, subtly.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice what this means. If trust is the foundation of everything, whoever controls the system controls that trust. A central bank can create more units of currency. A government can change the tax rules. I'm not saying this is bad by definition. I'm saying it's a human choice, made by human institutions, and those choices reach everyone's wallet. As long as the three pillars are standing, the note is worth something. If one of them starts to tremble, the conversation changes.

**[CENA 5]** A fifty-euro note next to a bank card. Underneath, the sentence appears gradually: "Money is a promise."
**NARRAÇÃO:** The first big consequence of money being a promise is called inflation. You'll see it in the next lesson, with a coffee and a simple sum anyone can do in their head. For now, keep this idea: promises can be kept or broken, slowly and quietly.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben with an open notebook on the table. A two-minute timer appears in the corner of the screen.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before we finish, a two-minute exercise. Take a note, or look at your bank balance. Ask yourself: what do I believe in, to accept this as value? Write the answer by hand. There's no right answer. The goal is for you to realise that you already trust many things without thinking about it.

**Texto no ecrã:** swap · double coincidence of wants · trust · promise

## Aula 1.2 — Inflation and the coffee (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "If you keep a thousand euros under the mattress for ten years, what can they buy at the end?"

**[CENA 1]** A coffee in a cup, with a one-euro price tag. The tag starts to rise, cent by cent, like a counter ticking.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's do a sum. A coffee costs one euro today. It sounds like very little, and it is very little. Now imagine prices rise four percent a year. This is a round, hypothetical number, just so we can do the maths. It's an illustrative example, nothing more. After one year, the coffee costs one euro and four cents. After five years, it costs about one euro and twenty-two. After ten years, it reaches one euro and forty-eight. Nobody notices anything special. It's a cent here, another there, and suddenly the same coffee costs almost half as much again.

**[CENA 2]** Animated line chart: the price of the coffee rising year by year, with the 1, 5 and 10 year marks highlighted.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now think about your money. If you have a thousand euros sitting in a drawer, ten years from now you still have a thousand euros on paper. But those thousand euros no longer buy what they used to. To keep being able to buy in ten years what a thousand euros buys today, you'd need about one thousand four hundred and eighty euros. Put the other way round: today's thousand euros are worth, in purchasing power, about six hundred and seventy-six euros.

**[CENA 3]** Two bars side by side. The first, full, labelled "1000 €". The second, shorter, labelled "≈676 € of purchasing power", with the difference slowly disappearing bit by bit.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice that this sum doesn't need a special year or a crisis. A small, steady inflation is enough. A rate of two percent, which is the European Central Bank's medium-term reference, already cuts money's purchasing power in half in about thirty-six years. Nobody feels that in a month. You feel it over a whole lifetime.

Look closely: the number in the drawer hasn't changed. What changed is what it can pay for. Economists call this purchasing power. And this is the real enemy of anyone who keeps money idle. It isn't a villain with a name and an address. It's an effect that always reaches the same person: the one whose money sits still.

**[CENA 4]** Split screen: on the left, the formula "72 ÷ 4 = 18", on the right an hourglass emptying to the halfway mark.
**NARRAÇÃO:** There's a practical rule worth memorising. It's called the rule of 72. You divide 72 by the inflation rate, and you get, in years, how long it takes for money to lose half its purchasing power. At four percent, that's eighteen years. It's an approximation, but it gives you the order of magnitude. And the order of magnitude is what you need to decide well. When you hear someone say inflation "isn't anything anymore", come back to this sum. A small number, repeated for years, is a huge force.

**[CENA 5]** Illustrative example: Inês, twenty-nine years old, a nurse in Coimbra, with a simple portrait and a drawn ID card, sitting at her desk with three thousand euros in a current account.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's be honest. Inflation isn't anyone's plan to harm you. It's a phenomenon that results from many decisions: demand, costs, monetary policy. But its effect always arrives the same way. Inês had three thousand euros in a current account earning practically nothing. She didn't want to risk everything, and she didn't want to watch her money shrink either. The first thing she did was understand the problem, not choose a product. That's the step I want you to take too. Before any decision, know how much money you have, how long you can leave it idle, and what the effect of inflation is in that specific case.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben with a calculator on screen. A list of four everyday prices appears: bread, petrol, a transport pass, part of the rent.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This matters for the rest of the bootcamp, because it's the first reason people look for alternatives. It's not because they want to get rich quickly. It's because they want their savings not to melt away in silence. So throughout this bootcamp, whenever we talk about returns, you'll ask one simple question: is it a gain above inflation, or just a nice-looking number on the screen? But be careful: looking for an alternative doesn't mean any alternative will do. Some assets rise a lot and fall even faster. In the next lessons, we'll see how to tell them apart.

**Texto no ecrã:** inflation · purchasing power · the rule of 72 · illustrative example
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise for today: pick a price you pay every week and work out what it would cost in ten years with a four percent yearly rise. Check question: if your money doesn't grow, what is it doing over time?

## Aula 1.3 — 2008: why Bitcoin was born (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "In October 2008, a short document appeared that changed the conversation about money. Let's look at the context."

**[CENA 1]** Stylised archive image, in greys and gold: a city skyline with the lights going out, one by one.
**NARRAÇÃO:** In 2008, the financial world was in crisis. Large banks had lent money to people who couldn't repay it, and the problem spread through the whole system. Governments and central banks rushed to rescue institutions. Many people lost their home, their job or their savings. It was in this context that many people started asking whether the traditional financial system was the only possible path.

**[CENA 2]** Screen with the title of a document being typed slowly: "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System". Below it, the date: 31 October 2008.
**NARRAÇÃO:** On 31 October 2008, a document with that title appeared on the internet. It was signed by a person using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. To this day, nobody knows for sure who that person is, or whether it's a group. That's the first strange thing, and it's worth keeping in mind, because it forces us to judge the idea and not the person.

**[CENA 3]** A small diagram: two people exchanging a coin. Between them, a bank counter fades away, with a line crossing it out.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The central idea was simple to say and hard to do. Money that could be sent from one person to another without going through a bank, without an intermediary approving every transfer. Instead of relying on trust in an institution, it relied on a rule written in code, which the whole network checked. That's the difference to hold on to: trust in a person versus trust in a process.

**[CENA 4]** Timeline with two highlighted dates: "3 January 2009" and, underneath, a golden square block labelled "genesis block".
**NARRAÇÃO:** On 3 January 2009, the network created the first block, called the genesis block. Inside that block, the author left a message: a headline from a British newspaper about bank bailouts. You don't need to interpret it much. Whoever wrote it wanted to make clear the context in which the idea was born, and that tells you something about the distrust that animated it at the time. It isn't an absolute truth about anyone's intentions, but it's a historical fact that explains a lot of the discourse you hear today.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben sitting on a park bench, looking at the camera. Text in the corner: "Telling the story is not predicting the price."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's be clear about what I'm doing here. I'm telling a story. If you want context, think of it this way: back then, for most people, money meant a bank. Anyone who wanted to transfer value to the other side of the world depended on banks, on delays and on fees. The proposal was to cut out that middleman, and not much more than that.

I'm not saying Bitcoin is good or bad, or that it will go up or down. That kind of prediction isn't part of this bootcamp, and anyone who makes it deserves suspicion. The story is there so you understand the problem the idea tried to solve. Merits and risks, we'll assess later, calmly. If you're curious about reading the original document, read it with the care you'd give a contract. The tone is technical, sober, and not very interested in convincing anyone.

**[CENA 6]** Three animated icons side by side: a payment arrow, a safe and a line chart. Each lights up in turn.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The problem the document tries to solve has a name: double spending. With a digital file, copying costs nothing. If you send a photo to someone, you still have it. With digital money, the question is different: how do you guarantee that the same unit isn't spent twice, without a bank confirming every operation? That question is older than Bitcoin, and it's the key to understanding everything else.

And here's the part I want you to remember. Bitcoin wasn't born to be an investment. It was born as a proposal for money that doesn't depend on a single point of control. Today, many people use it as a store of value, others as a means of payment, others still for speculation. All three coexist, and the difference between them is huge. When someone talks to you about Bitcoin, always ask which of the three they have in mind.

**[CENA 7]** An on-screen exercise, with a lined sheet of paper and a pen writing.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Reflection exercise: write on one line what you think an intermediary does when it approves a transfer. Then write what you lose if it doesn't exist. There's no right answer. The goal is for you to see what Bitcoin tries to replace, and what that replacement costs.

**Texto no ecrã:** 2008 · trust in processes · genesis block · store of value, payment, speculation

## Aula 1.4 — The notebook nobody erases (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Imagine a notebook that the whole village has, and that nobody can tear up without everyone noticing."

**[CENA 1]** A simply drawn village, with a square and several houses. In each house, an open notebook with the same handwriting.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Imagine a village where everyone has an account book. Every time someone owes money to another person, a line is written: who paid, who to, how much, and the date. Only here there's one rule. There isn't a single notebook kept by the head of the parish council. Everyone has a copy, and the copies are updated together. That is, in very simple terms, a blockchain: a chain of blocks, where each block is a page with several lines.

**[CENA 2]** Animated graphic `blockchain-caderno`: one line, one page, a chain of pages linked together. Each line of text appears inside a page.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's go step by step. First, the lines. A transaction says: this wallet sent so many units of this currency to that wallet. Second, the pages. A block bundles several transactions and closes when it's full, or when a certain amount of time has passed. Third, the link between pages. Each page has a kind of seal that results from the content of the page before it. Technically it's called a hash function, but think of it as a fingerprint of the content: if the content changes, the fingerprint changes. If someone alters an old line, the seal no longer matches, and all the following pages become invalid.

**[CENA 3]** Animation: someone tries to erase a line on an old page. The page turns red, and the following pages turn grey, one by one, like dominoes.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is what makes the notebook hard to erase. It's not that changing anything is impossible. It's that changing it means redoing the whole path, and it has to be accepted by the majority of the network. And that majority has copies of the notebook. Anyone trying to tamper with an old page has to convince the whole village that the story is different. In practice, that doesn't happen.

Also notice something important: someone using the network doesn't need to trust whoever writes. They need to trust the rules, which are public and can be checked by anyone. It's a change in attitude. Instead of asking who you are, you ask what the rules allow.

**[CENA 4]** Two screens side by side. On the left, a computer solving a puzzle. On the right, a set of coins locked as collateral. Underneath, the labels "proof of work" and "proof of stake".
**NARRAÇÃO:** But then, who writes the new page? This is where many people find things confusing, and I'll simplify it. There are two main methods. The first is called proof of work, or mining. Computers compete to solve a difficult mathematical problem. Whoever solves it first writes the next page and receives a reward. It uses a lot of energy, and it's the method Bitcoin uses. The second is called proof of stake. Instead of competing with calculation, participants lock up coins as collateral, and the system chooses who writes the page. If that person tries to cheat, they lose the collateral. This is the method the Ethereum network adopted in September 2022, in a transition called The Merge.

**[CENA 5]** An animated scale: on one pan, "energy", on the other, "collateral". Both pans stay in balance, with an arrow saying "nobody writes without paying".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice what the two have in common. Nobody writes in the notebook without paying a cost, whether it's energy or collateral. And nobody decides on their own what is true. That's the big idea behind a blockchain: a shared record without a central authority having the last word.

**[CENA 6]** A highlighted warning, gold on charcoal, with a discreet exclamation mark: "Solid technology is not the same as a good project."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now an important warning. A blockchain solves a specific problem: keeping a shared record without a central entity. That doesn't mean the project is safe, or that the currency is good, or that the price will rise. Solid technology can be attached to a badly designed product. We'll separate these things throughout the course, and from module 2 onwards you'll have tools to do that.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative example: Inês explaining the four-page notebook to her husband, who works in logistics, with the pages linked by arrows.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative example, to close. Inês wanted to understand this with her husband, who works in logistics, and he asked her why nobody could erase the notebook. She answered with a simple three-step exercise: draw four pages, with two lines each, write on each seal a word that summed up the previous page, and then change one line on the first page to see what happened to the others. He got it in two minutes. You'll need a little more, and that's fine. You don't need to understand the maths to understand the logic. You just need to see why changing the past is so costly.

**Texto no ecrã:** shared notebook · block · seal · proof of work · proof of stake

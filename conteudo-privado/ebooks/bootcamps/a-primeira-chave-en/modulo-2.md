# Módulo 2 — The crypto map (≈17 min)
**Objetivo:** tell apart Bitcoin, Ethereum, stablecoins, altcoins and memecoins, and assess a project with seven questions.  **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) explain the 21 million cap and the halving; 2) tell Ethereum apart from Bitcoin, and a stablecoin from a speculative coin; 3) apply the seven questions to a project.

## Aula 2.1 — Bitcoin: 21 million and the halving (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "There's a coin that will only ever exist in 21 million units. Sounds like good news. But there's a detail: how do those units come into existence?"

**[CENA 1]** A golden digital safe on a charcoal background. A counter in the corner shows 0 and slowly climbs towards a limit marked in red: "21,000,000".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Bitcoin has a programmed limit: twenty-one million units, at most. That number wasn't chosen by chance, and it doesn't change by the decision of one person or one company. It is written into the protocol, and anyone who uses the network accepts that rule when they use it. It sounds simple, but it has an important consequence: the quantity of coins never grows without limit, and that is different from almost every currency you know.

But how do the units enter circulation? Every time a miner writes a new page in the notebook, they receive a reward, in bitcoin. At the beginning, that reward was fifty units per block. That is where all the coins that exist today come from.

**[CENA 2]** An animated clock with the caption "210,000 blocks ≈ 4 years". Each time the clock completes a turn, the reward is cut in half, with a small click.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is the halving, which means cut in half. Every two hundred and ten thousand blocks, the reward is cut in half. Since blocks come out, on average, every ten minutes, this happens roughly every four years. It isn't exact, because the pace varies a little, but it's the right order of magnitude for you to see the pattern.

**[CENA 3]** Animated graphic `halving`: a descending staircase. Each step shows the date and the reward per block. The last step blinks.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's go through the sequence, which is what matters. Fifty units per block until 2012. Then twenty-five. In 2016, twelve point five. In 2020, six point two five. In 2024, three point one two five. Notice the pattern. Each cut slows the pace at which new units are created. The more time passes, the fewer new coins come in. That's why people who see Bitcoin as a store of value use this argument: it's a supply that shrinks over time, unlike currencies that central banks can issue with no technical limit.

**[CENA 4]** An animated scale: on one pan, "less new supply"; on the other, "demand, news, mood, regulation". The scale swings, without settling.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now the counterpoint, because there has to be one. Less new supply doesn't guarantee a high price. The price depends on how many people want to buy, how many want to sell, news, mood, regulation, and everything else. Think of a limited edition of a book: the print run is fixed, but nobody guarantees that readers will show up. A cut in the reward is one factor among many. History shows that the effect on the price is neither automatic nor predictable, and anyone who sells you a certainty on this point is selling you a fantasy.

**[CENA 5]** A sum on screen, step by step: "50 + 25 + 12.5 + 6.25 + …" and, underneath, "× 210,000 = 21,000,000".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's do the sum, so you can see the number is real. If you add fifty, twenty-five, twelve point five, and so on, each period lasts two hundred and ten thousand blocks. If you multiply the sum of the rewards by that number of blocks, you get roughly twenty-one million. That's why the limit is what it is. It isn't a round number chosen to look nice; it's the result of a series that keeps shrinking. If you do the sum on your phone's calculator, you'll see the result approaches twenty-one million and never goes past it.

**[CENA 6]** Illustrative example: a cake with one hundred slices. Each person who helps bake receives ten slices, then five, then two and a half. The remaining slices are marked in grey.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative example, with round, invented numbers. Imagine a cake that only has one hundred slices. In the first days, each person who helps bake gets ten slices. Then they get five. Then two and a half. The cake never goes past one hundred slices, and new slices become rarer and rarer. But this says nothing about whether the cake is good, or whether it's worth eating. Scarcity is a property, not an investment thesis.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative example: Tiago, twenty-eight years old, a programmer in Braga, making notes in a notebook beside his laptop.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative example: Tiago, twenty-eight years old, a programmer in Braga, understood this argument and found it reasonable. But he didn't stop there. He asked himself who was buying, why, and what would happen if those people stopped buying. That's the question I want you to keep in mind throughout this module. Scarcity explains one part of the story. Demand explains the other.

## Aula 2.2 — Ethereum: the village with rules that enforce themselves (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Bitcoin is a notebook of accounts. Ethereum tries to be a whole village, with rules that enforce themselves."

**[CENA 1]** Two notebooks side by side. The first has a single column, "who has what". The second has columns, functions and small blocks of code.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Bitcoin was designed, above all, to record who has what. Ethereum launched in 2015 with a different ambition: to let anyone write rules into the notebook, and to have those rules run by themselves, without an intermediary. It isn't just a record of balances. It's a kind of shared computer, where many people run programmes at the same time, and everyone confirms the result.

**[CENA 2]** An animated vending machine. A coin goes in, the selection lights up, the product drops. Above it, the caption: "if this, then that".
**NARRAÇÃO:** These rules are called smart contracts. The name is a bit misleading. They aren't contracts in the legal sense, and they aren't smart in the sense of thinking. They are programmes that run on the network. A helpful analogy is the vending machine. You insert a coin, choose an option, and the machine hands over the product. You don't need an employee to confirm it. The rule is inside, and it applies the same way to everyone. A smart contract works like this: if this happens, then that happens. When the condition is met, the action executes. Each of these programmes has the same skeleton: a rule, a condition and an automatic execution. The difference lies in what gets executed, and in who is responsible if something goes wrong.

**[CENA 3]** Four small icons: a loan, an exchange market, a game, a digital collection. Each has a question mark underneath.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This opens many doors. You can have automatic loans, exchange markets between people, games, digital collections. I won't tell you which of these will work. Not all of them do, and some should never have existed. Just because something is technically possible doesn't mean it's a good idea to use it, or that it has any real use for you.

**[CENA 4]** A contract written on a screen, with a red line and a flashing warning: "error in code".
**NARRAÇÃO:** And here comes the warning that is worth its weight in gold: code can fail too. A contract written in a hurry can contain a mistake, and mistakes on a blockchain are hard to correct, because the notebook can't be torn out. In 2016, a project called The DAO lost an enormous amount of ether because of a flaw in its code, and that led to a split of the network itself. I don't want to scare you with numbers. I want you to understand one thing: code is not a guarantee of anything on its own.

**[CENA 5]** A timeline: 2015 labelled "launch", September 2022 labelled "The Merge", and between the two an arrow changing colour from red to gold.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now let's talk about the big change in 2022. Until September of that year, Ethereum used proof of work, like Bitcoin. Then it moved to proof of stake. That transition is called The Merge. The network stopped depending on computers competing through calculation, and started depending on participants who lock up coins as collateral. The change altered how the network uses energy and how new coins are issued. But it didn't remove the need to understand what you're buying. A better technology doesn't fix a badly designed project.

**[CENA 6]** A wallet screen with a fee field flashing in yellow. Beside it, a label: "twenty euros sent, similar fee".
**NARRAÇÃO:** It's also worth understanding how Ethereum charges for operations. Every action on the network has a cost, called a fee, and that fee varies with demand. At busy moments, it can become expensive. If you ever send a small amount and are surprised by the cost, now you know why. Illustrative example: Inês wanted to send twenty euros from one wallet to another, and the fee ended up almost as high as the transfer itself. It wasn't a tragedy. It was a lesson. Also think about this: when the network is very full, each operation competes with the others for space, and whoever pays more goes first. So the same transfer can cost little on a calm morning and a lot in a busy afternoon. Before any operation, always check how much it's going to cost you.

**[CENA 7]** Two columns on a board: "Bitcoin" and "Ethereum". Ruben writing, by hand, a question at the bottom: "do I need a notebook or a programme?"
**NARRAÇÃO:** Think of these as two different tools. A simple notebook is good for recording who owes what. A programme running on the network is good for executing complicated rules without an intermediary. Both are useful, and both have their own risks. The common mistake is to assume that complexity means quality. Often it's just more surface area for things to go wrong.

**Texto no ecrã:** 2015 · smart contract · The DAO · The Merge · network fee
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise: write, in two columns, what Bitcoin does well and what Ethereum tries to do on top. Then ask yourself when you need a simple notebook and when you need a programme running on the network. Check question: what sets a smart contract apart from a simple record of transfers?

## Aula 2.3 — Stablecoins: the digital dollar and the risk of whoever issues it (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "There's a crypto that is supposed to always be worth one dollar. It looks like a safe vault. Ask yourself: who holds the key to the vault?"

**[CENA 1]** A digital coin with the dollar symbol, sliding along a line that should be perfectly straight. The line trembles slightly.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A stablecoin is a digital coin that tries to hold a fixed value, normally one dollar. The best-known examples are USDC and USDT. It sounds like the ideal scenario: a digital dollar that moves across the network in seconds. But notice the word "tries". A stablecoin has no magic guarantee. On the contrary, its value depends on a concrete promise, and that promise has to be kept by someone.

**[CENA 2]** An illustration of a casino chip: a value written on its face, and behind it a small hand holding the promise.
**NARRAÇÃO:** What a stablecoin promises is something specific: that, for each unit issued, there is an equivalent reserve, in cash or in low-risk assets, and that the issuer redeems it at the exact value. Think of a casino chip, or a supermarket voucher. The chip is worth what is printed on it because the casino honours it. If the casino stops honouring it, the chip loses value. A stablecoin works in the same way: its value depends on who issues it. An everyday example: a company meal card is only worth what the restaurant accepts, and the restaurant only accepts it while the system keeps paying it. Trust runs down in layers, and one weak layer drags the others down with it.

**[CENA 3]** A diagram with three linked boxes: "reserves", "issuer", "redemption". A panic arrow, coming from many people, points to "redemption".
**NARRAÇÃO:** That's why there's a term you should know: issuer risk. Whoever issues the coin has to hold verifiable reserves, has to follow rules, and has to be able to redeem coins when people ask. If there are doubts about the reserves, or if many people try to redeem at the same time, the value can drift away from the dollar. That isn't a theoretical possibility. It's exactly the scenario that gets tested when the market panics.

**[CENA 4]** A line chart that rises to the right and, on one day, drops sharply. The caption says "loss of peg".
**NARRAÇÃO:** This has already happened. In 2022, an algorithmic stablecoin, from an ecosystem that at one point became enormous, lost its peg to the dollar and dragged a lot of value down with it. It was not a coin backed by dollar reserves. It was a balancing mechanism between two digital coins. When that balance broke, the value collapsed. This story is the best argument for not trusting the word "stable".

**[CENA 5]** A generic regulatory seal logo, with the abbreviation "MiCA" (Markets in Crypto-Assets) and a date: 30 December 2024.
**NARRAÇÃO:** In the European Union, there is now a legal framework called MiCA, which has applied to crypto-asset service providers since thirty December 2024. It brings more rules, more transparency and more supervision. It's a real step forward. But rules are not price guarantees, and it's important not to confuse the two. A stablecoin can comply with regulation and still carry risks that depend on its reserves, its liquidity and the trust of the people who use it. Notice a practical difference: a regulation obliges companies to meet their duties. It doesn't oblige the coin to hold a fixed price. They are different things, and one doesn't replace the other.

**[CENA 6]** Illustrative example: one hundred units of a stablecoin, with the value dropping from one dollar to ninety cents.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's look at an illustrative example, with round, invented numbers. Imagine you hold one hundred units of a stablecoin that is worth one dollar. If the coin loses its peg and drops to ninety cents, your balance is now worth ninety dollars. Nothing dramatic at first glance. But if you were using it as a safe haven, you've lost ten percent at the exact moment you were looking for security. That's what makes the example uncomfortable.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative example: Rui, forty-one years old, a teacher in Leiria, at his desk with a notepad and three questions written on it.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Why do people use stablecoins? To get out of volatile positions quickly, to send value between countries, to hold a digital dollar for a period of time. These are legitimate uses. But none of them removes the risk. Illustrative example: Rui, forty-one years old, a teacher in Leiria, had put part of his savings into a stablecoin, convinced it was idle money with no risk. When he asked where the reserves were and who audited them, he realised he had assumed something without checking it. He didn't lose everything, but he learned to ask questions. If you have doubts about how a value behaves, the first question is always the same: who is obliged to redeem it, and under what conditions?

**Texto no ecrã:** stablecoin · reserves · issuer risk · peg · MiCA
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise: pick a stablecoin you know and write three questions you would ask its issuer. Who is it, where are the reserves, and who can audit them? If you can't find clear answers, that is your answer. Check question: why does a stablecoin depend more on who issues it than on a technical promise?

## Aula 2.4 — Altcoins, memecoins and the seven questions (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "There are thousands of coins, and most of them will disappear. How do you tell a serious project from a joke with a logo?"

**[CENA 1]** A screen full of coin logos appearing and disappearing, like leaves in the wind.
**NARRAÇÃO:** We call altcoins all the cryptocurrencies that are not Bitcoin. There are many, with very different goals. Some try to be better payment systems, others want to connect data chains, others want to be platforms for applications. And then there are memecoins, which deserve a section of their own. A reminder: this is educational content, and not financial advice. The risk of loss is real, and in crypto it is often total.

**[CENA 2]** A coin drawn with a smiling face and the caption "born from a joke". Underneath, an attention bar that rises and falls quickly.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A memecoin is often born from a joke, an image or a social media trend. As a rule, it doesn't solve a concrete problem. Its value depends almost entirely on the attention it receives. When the attention changes, the price changes. This can happen in hours, sometimes in minutes. Think of a soap bubble at a birthday party: it's pretty, everyone points at it, and it bursts without warning. And notice a word that keeps coming up: attention. If the value comes from attention, whoever sells early wins, and whoever buys late loses.

**[CENA 3]** Split screen: on the left, an advert with stars and the phrase "guaranteed profit". On the right, the same phrase crossed out in red.
**NARRAÇÃO:** I'm not saying all of them are fraud. I'm saying the risk is extremely high and that, often, the marketing is the only product. When someone sells you a coin with a guaranteed profit, the right response is to be suspicious. No honest project can guarantee a return, and anyone who says it can is trying to stop you asking questions.

**[CENA 4]** A panel with seven numbers, from one to seven, in a row. Each number lights up when Ruben talks about it.
**NARRAÇÃO:** So that you don't rely on instinct, I'm going to give you a tool: seven questions. They aren't a formula, they're a filter. If you can't answer one of them, you're not ready to buy. First: what problem does this project solve? If the answer is just "it's going to go up", that's a bad sign. Second: who is behind it, and is that public? An anonymous team isn't necessarily fraud, but it raises the risk, and you need to know that before deciding. A project with identified people who publicly stand behind what they do carries a different kind of risk. Neither of them is free of risk.

**[CENA 5]** A pie chart: one very large slice in dark grey, labelled "one wallet", and small slices for the rest.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Third: what is the total supply, and who holds it? If a small number of wallets hold most of the coins, the price can be manipulated easily. Fourth: is there real utility, or only speculation? Ask who uses the project for anything other than buying and selling. Fifth: who controls the code, and can they change it? If one person can change the rules without warning, you need to know that before you get in. A supplementary question worth asking: who decides on updates, and how are those decisions communicated?

**[CENA 6]** An exit door labelled "liquidity". The door is narrow, and in front of it many people are pushing at the same time.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Sixth: where is it traded, and is there enough liquidity to sell? An asset without buyers is an asset you can't sell at the price you see on the screen. Seventh, and perhaps the most useful: what happens if the project disappears tomorrow? If the answer is "nothing, I lose everything", you already know how much you can risk. If you have no way of selling, the chart on the screen is just an opinion. These seven questions won't give you certainties. They'll give you better questions than the ones the advertising asks you.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative example: a card with four lines marked in red and a final stamp: "don't enter".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative example, with invented numbers. Imagine a coin that promises high yield, has a founder with no face, holds ninety percent of its supply in a single wallet, and has no use outside the coin itself. You've answered badly to almost all of them. You don't need anything more to decide not to enter. And if, on the other hand, the answers were good, they still wouldn't oblige you to buy. A good answer only tells you it's worth studying more, never that the price will go up. Also watch out for requests: if someone asks you to send crypto to "unlock" a sum of money, or pressures you to decide today, that isn't analysis. It's pressure. We'll talk about scams in more detail in module 5. Notice that I haven't asked you to get it right. I've asked you not to be carried away by haste, and to understand what you don't know yet.

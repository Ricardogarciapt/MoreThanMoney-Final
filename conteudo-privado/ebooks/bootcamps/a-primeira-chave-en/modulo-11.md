# Módulo 11 — Perpetuals for real (≈17 min)
**Objetivo:** understand what a perpetual is before you touch one: the funding you pay, the margin you choose, the liquidation that can happen and the costs that do not appear on the buy button. That way, leverage is a choice, not an impulse.  **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) explain funding and who pays whom; 2) choose between isolated and cross margin and say why; 3) calculate, in simplified form, a liquidation price; 4) add up the fees and the real cost of a trade; 5) size a position by the stop and not by the leverage.

**Production note:** all the numbers are illustrative examples and are said out loud and shown on screen. Do not show real balances. The account panel is recorded with a simulated account and test values. Perpetuals carry a risk of losing the value you put up as margin, and nothing that is recorded may suggest otherwise.

## Aula 11.1 — What a perpetual is, and funding (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "A perpetual is a contract that never expires. It sounds simple, and the part that does not expire is exactly the part that will cost you attention."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben in a medium shot, almost black background, details in gold. Text on screen: “Educational content. The risk of loss is real.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before any numbers, the warning that will be on screen throughout this module: educational content. The risk of loss is real. With perpetuals you can lose everything you put up as margin, and that happens faster than most people think. This module is for understanding, not for starting. Level three is optional. And before any real euro, what counts is the simulated account, as you have known since the start of the bootcamp. With that said, let us look at what a perpetual is. I want you to leave here with one clear sentence.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: a contract with an endless arrow and the words “no expiry date”. Text on screen: “perpetual = no date”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A normal futures contract has an expiry date. At the end of that date it is settled, and that is the end of it. A perpetual has no such date. It stays open for as long as you want it to, and as long as you have margin to keep it open. To stay close to the spot price, the perpetual uses a mechanism called funding. It is not a brokerage fee. It is a payment that happens from time to time, between those who are long and those who are short. When the perpetual trades above the spot price, those who are long pay those who are short. When it trades below, the opposite happens. It is the part most people ignore, and it weighs more than it seems on positions that stay open for days.

**[CENA 3]** Table on screen: “Illustrative example · long position of €1,000 notional · funding of 0.05% per period”. Ruben points at the table.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let us look at an illustrative example. You have a long position with one thousand euros of notional value, which is the total size of the position, not the money you put in. The funding is zero point zero five per cent per payment period. Per period, you pay fifty cents. If the funding is positive, those who are long pay. If it is negative, those who are long receive. The number is only there to make the sum, and funding changes over time. What matters is the mechanism: someone who stays in a position for a long time with the funding working against them pays a small amount every day, and that does not show up on the candlestick chart.

**[CENA 4]** Split screen: on the left the perpetual's chart, on the right the spot price chart, with the gap between the two flashing. Text on screen: “perpetual vs spot price”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Why does the perpetual stay close to the spot price? Because funding pushes. When there are many people long and the perpetual trades above the real price, funding turns positive, and those who are long pay to keep their position. That tends to cool demand on the long side. The reverse happens when many people are short: funding turns negative, and the shorts are the ones who pay. It is not magic, it is an incentive. In practice, funding is a cost or an income that runs while the position is open, and its sign changes with the market's conditions.

**[CENA 5]** Exercise sheet with a three-day timeline and fields “funding paid” for each day. Ruben with the pen in his hand.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Two-minute exercise, on paper. A long position of one thousand euros of notional, with funding of zero point zero five per cent per period, and three periods a day, which is one of the common formats. How much do you pay in twenty-four hours? Three periods of fifty cents make one euro and fifty cents. One euro and fifty cents a day, on a notional of one thousand. An illustrative example, of course. Now repeat the sum with the position open for a whole week. If the funding works against you every day, how much do you pay in total? Do the sum without a calculator, and only then check it. Next lesson we look at what happens to your margin when things go wrong.

**Texto no ecrã:** perpetual = no date · funding = payment between positions · illustrative example · you pay while the position is open
**Check question:** You are long and funding is positive. Who pays? Answer: you, as the long side. With positive funding, the payment goes from those who buy to those who sell.

## Aula 11.2 — Isolated margin and cross margin (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Two accounts with the same balance can react in completely different ways to the same fall. The difference lies in a choice that almost nobody reads."

**[CENA 1]** Screen of a simulated margin panel, with two options, “isolated” and “cross”. Text on screen: “margin: what protects you and what puts you at risk”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Margin is the money you put up to hold the position. It is not the cost of the whole position, it is the collateral. When you open a leveraged perpetual, the platform requires an initial margin, and it is that margin which rises and falls with the market. There are two ways to organise it: isolated and cross. The difference is not in the leverage. It is in who pays when the position goes wrong. This choice sounds technical, but it is one of the most important risk decisions in this module, and it is often the one people make without thinking.

**[CENA 2]** Animation with two boxes. In isolated, a small box with a wall around it, holding only one position. In cross, a large box with several positions and one shared balance.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Isolated margin means each position has its own collateral. If you put one hundred euros into a position, only those one hundred euros are at risk. If the position is liquidated, you lose those hundred, and the rest of the account stays intact. Cross margin means all positions share the available balance. A position in trouble can use the money from the others to hold on. It sounds like an advantage, and sometimes it is, but it has a cost: a single bad position can drag the entire account down. For someone learning, that is a big problem.

**[CENA 3]** Comparison table on screen: “isolated: limit per position · cross: shares the balance”. Ruben points at the risk row.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let us put the two side by side, with an illustrative example. You have a simulated account of one thousand euros and open two positions. With isolated margin, you give one hundred euros to each. If the first goes wrong and is liquidated, you lose those hundred, and the second carries on with its hundred. With cross margin, both use the full balance of one thousand. If the first goes wrong, the second can use what is left, and liquidation only happens when the whole account reaches the limit. In other words, cross holds out for longer, but when it fails, it fails all together. The numbers are for the account, not for any real position.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben talking to camera, holding a card. Text on screen: “Cross does not protect you. It only delays.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is my view, which you can argue with: for someone starting out, isolated margin is the choice I recommend. Someone starting has not yet tested a risk plan, and isolated forces you to decide how much each position gets. That limit is a lesson in itself. With cross, the account works like one single pot, and it is easy to give more margin to a position that is losing, in the hope that it recovers. That is called putting more money into something that is already going wrong. Cross has its place for those who already have written and tested rules, but it is not where you start.

**[CENA 5]** Exercise sheet with two columns, “isolated” and “cross”, and one field: “what happens to the other position if the first goes wrong?”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Three-minute exercise. If the platform you use allows it, do it in a simulated account: open two small positions with the same leverage, one in isolated margin and one in cross. If it does not allow it, do it on paper. Fill in the sheet: how much money is inside each position, what happens to the other one if the first goes wrong, and which of the two leaves you more at ease. There is no right answer to the last question, only yours. Next lesson we see what liquidation is and how it is calculated, in simplified form, with an illustrative sum.

**Texto no ecrã:** isolated: the risk stays in the position · cross: the risk belongs to the whole account · choose before you open
**Check question:** With cross margin, what can a position with problems use to hold on? Answer: the balance of the other positions, and so the risk becomes the risk of the whole account.
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** "Next lesson: liquidation, with an illustrative sum."

## Aula 11.3 — Liquidation, with an illustrative sum (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Liquidation is the moment when the platform closes your position because there is no longer enough collateral. Nobody asks you. It just happens."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben with a notebook open to a page with a sum in pencil. Text on screen: “Illustrative example”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let us work through the liquidation sum step by step, with an illustrative sum. It is not anyone's actual account. These are round numbers, so you can see the mechanics. Imagine a simulated account with five hundred euros of margin on a long position, with ten times leverage. Ten times means the position has a notional value of five thousand euros. The five hundred is your collateral, the five thousand is the size of the bet. This difference is the centre of this lesson. And notice: the example is illustrative, and the platform you use may have different numbers and different rules.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: a margin bar filling with gold, with a red line that slowly drops as the price falls. Text on screen: “margin being consumed”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** When the price moves against your position, the loss comes out of the margin. With five thousand of notional, every one per cent move against you is worth fifty euros. If the price falls ten per cent, you lose five hundred, and the margin drops to zero. In a simplified account, that is where liquidation happens: when the loss consumes the margin. But this is a simplification, and the difference matters. In practice, the platform requires a minimum maintenance margin, and it liquidates before the margin reaches zero. So the real liquidation price sits a little closer to the entry than the simple sum says.

**[CENA 3]** Table on screen: “entry 100 · simplified liquidation ≈ 90 · illustrative example”. Ruben points at the numbers.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now the price. Imagine the position is long with an entry at one hundred. With this simplified sum, the liquidation sits near ninety, because a ten per cent fall is what the five hundred of margin can absorb against the five thousand of notional. Notice the logic behind it: roughly one divided by the leverage. Ten times gives ten per cent. Twenty times gives five per cent. The higher the leverage, the shorter the distance to liquidation. In other words, leverage does not buy you more time. It shortens your margin for error. This figure is an illustrative example, and it is not a rule for any position.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben talking to camera, with the line on screen: “Liquidation is not a number. It is a date that arrives without warning.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** And now the point I want you to keep. Liquidation does not warn you. There is no moment when the platform tells you it is about to close, and there is no guarantee that the price stops exactly at the value you calculated. In a fast market, with one long candle and suddenly, the price can pass the liquidation level in seconds, and the close happens at whatever price there is at that moment, which can be worse than the number you wrote down. So the liquidation price you see on screen is a reference limit, not a protection. Protection, if it exists, is somewhere else: it is the stop, which we will look at later in this level.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben closing the notebook. Card on screen: “Recalculate before you open.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** Mission for this lesson: take an illustrative account, in a simulation, and calculate the simplified liquidation for three leverage levels, five, ten and twenty. Write down the percentage distance to the liquidation price in each case. Then check it on the panel of the platform you use, if it has one. If the numbers do not match, look for the difference, which is usually in the maintenance margin and the fees. Do not open any real position to do this calculation, not even out of curiosity. Next lesson we talk about costs, which is where the sum usually turns out to be more expensive than it looks.

**Texto no ecrã:** notional = margin × leverage · liquidation arrives without warning · illustrative example · leverage shortens the margin for error
**Check question:** With the same margin of five hundred euros, what happens to the liquidation price when you move from ten to twenty times? Answer: it moves closer to the entry, because the same collateral supports a larger notional.
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** "Next lesson: fees, the real cost of a trade."

## Aula 11.4 — Fees and the real cost (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "A trade can look as though it went well, and once you do the sum on the fees, nothing is left. This lesson is about the sums nobody does in front of the screen."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben with an invoice sheet, title “illustrative invoice”. Text on screen: “costs you do not see”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let us talk about money that leaves without being seen. With perpetuals there are at least three kinds of cost. The opening fee, the closing fee and the funding we have already seen. There is also a hidden cost, which is the spread, the difference between the buy price and the sell price at that moment. Each one is small, but added up and multiplied by the leverage and by how often you trade, they can eat a large part of what you thought you had. This invoice is illustrative, with no values from any particular platform. Your authorised platform has its own fee tables, and you have to read them before you open anything.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: an entry arrow and an exit arrow, each with a small slice cut off. Text on screen: “maker · taker”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The fee depends on how you enter. When you place an order that waits in the order book, and is not executed straight away, you usually pay a lower fee, called maker. When the order executes immediately and takes liquidity from the market, you pay a higher fee, the taker fee. Platforms vary, so check the fee menu of your authorised platform before you open, for each type of order. Also ask yourself whether the order type you are using is the one you want, and not simply the fastest one to click.

**[CENA 3]** Table on screen: “notional €5,000 · fee of 0.05% per side · illustrative example”. Ruben points at the numbers.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative example, with round numbers. A notional of five thousand euros, and a fee of zero point zero five per cent on each side of the trade, opening and closing. Opening costs two euros and fifty cents. Closing costs another two euros and fifty cents. Total, five euros, on a trade of five thousand. That seems little. Now look at the margin: five hundred euros. Five euros on five hundred is one per cent of the collateral, in fees alone, before the market has moved. If you repeat this trade ten times with the same margin, that is fifty euros, ten per cent of the collateral, going to the platform. None of those fifty euros appears on the chart.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben talking to camera, with the simulated account panel appearing beside him. Text on screen: “fee cost for the size of the account”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is where the account panel of Aurum Flow Cripto helps. It shows the notional, the useful leverage and the fee cost for the size of your account, before you open anything. The question you should always ask is this: if the trade does exactly what the plan says, how much is left after the fees? If the answer is almost nothing, the trade may not be worth the effort, however good it looks on the chart. This is not a guarantee of anything. It is a sum done in your head, and it protects you from an illusion.

**[CENA 5]** Exercise sheet with fields: “notional”, “opening fee”, “closing fee”, “total cost”, “as % of margin”. Ruben pointing at the first field.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Five-minute exercise, on paper. A margin of one thousand euros, leverage of five times, and a fee of zero point zero five per cent per side. What is the notional? Five thousand. How much does opening cost? Two euros and fifty cents. How much does closing cost? Another two euros and fifty cents. What is the total cost, as a percentage of the margin? Half a per cent. Now repeat it with leverage of twenty times, keeping the same margin. You do not need to get your platform's fee exactly right. You need to understand that the cost rises with the notional, not with the margin you put in. Next lesson we look at the most important part of this module: why leverage does not change the right risk.

**Texto no ecrã:** notional · fee per side · total cost · % of margin · illustrative example
**Check question:** Why do fees rise when you increase the leverage, with the same margin? Answer: because the fee is calculated on the notional, and the notional rises with the leverage.

## Aula 11.5 — Leverage does not change the right risk (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Leverage sounds like an accelerator. But if it were only an accelerator, what would stop the car? The answer is the stop and the size of the position, not the number on the screen."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben in a medium shot, almost black background. Text on screen: “Risk is defined by the stop and the size”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** We have reached the lesson that sums up level three, and perhaps the most important one in the module. When someone tells you they will use five times, or twenty times, the right question is not what the leverage is. The right question is: how much do I lose if the stop is hit? That question has an answer that does not depend on the leverage. It depends on two things: the distance to the stop and the size of the position. Leverage only tells you how much margin you need to hold that position. We will see why, with sums and with illustrative examples, of course.

**[CENA 2]** Animation with a formula on screen: “loss = notional × stop distance”. Text on screen: “risk = notional × stop”.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The formula fits on a napkin: the loss, if the stop is hit, equals the notional multiplied by the stop distance as a percentage. That is all. Leverage does not enter into it. What enters is the size of the position, which is the notional, and the point where you are willing to get out. With this, the risk per trade is defined before you open. If you want to lose at most one per cent of your account, you choose the notional and the stop distance so that the loss, in that case, is one per cent. Leverage then becomes a consequence of that choice, not the starting point.

**[CENA 3]** Three columns on screen with the same sum: “account: €1,000 · risk: €10 (1%)”. Illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let us take the same account of one thousand euros, with a risk of ten euros per trade, which is one per cent. First case: a stop at two per cent from the entry price. Notional: ten divided by two per cent gives five hundred euros. Leverage, against the account, half a time. Second case: a stop at one per cent. Notional of one thousand euros, leverage of one time. Third case: a stop at half a per cent. Notional of two thousand, leverage of two times. In all three, if the stop is hit, you lose exactly ten euros. What changed was the notional and the leverage, not the risk. This is the point I want you to keep.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben with the notebook open. Card on screen: “A stop is not a guarantee.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** An important caveat: the stop is an order, and an order can be executed at a worse price than the one you wrote, if the market makes a sudden jump. In a sharp move, the close can come out further away than the level you marked. So the stop reduces the risk, but does not eliminate it. And liquidation still exists, because if the margin is too small for the stop distance, the platform closes the position before the stop is reached. The rule is not “I set a stop and I can relax”. The rule is: the stop sits far enough away for normal noise, and the position is small enough that a jump does not wipe out your account.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben closing the notebook. Text on screen: “Level 3: simulated account only, up to 30 trades with a journal.”
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now the level three rule, which is not negotiable. This level is done only in a simulated account, MTM Funded or WebTrader, until you have completed thirty trades with a full journal. Thirty, not three. The journal records the date, the plan, the stop, the notional, the risk in euros, the fee, the reason for entry, the reason for exit and what you learned. Only after thirty trades can you think about a real account, and that decision is yours, never made with rent money. Mission for this lesson: open your simulated account, do the risk calculation with the model from this lesson, record the first lines of the journal, and write at the top of the sheet: zero of thirty.

**Texto no ecrã:** risk = notional × stop distance · leverage is margin, not risk · a stop is not a guarantee · 30 trades in simulation
**Check question:** With one thousand euros and a risk of ten euros, what happens to the risk if you change a one per cent stop to half a per cent? Answer: the notional rises to two thousand and the leverage to two times, but the loss, if the stop is hit, is still ten euros.

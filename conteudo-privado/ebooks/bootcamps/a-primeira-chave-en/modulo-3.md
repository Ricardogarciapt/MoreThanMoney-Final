# Módulo 3 — Buying for the first time (≈17 min)
**Objetivo:** buy for the first time on an authorized platform, with the costs and the order type understood and a loss limit set.  **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) check whether a platform is authorized and explain KYC; 2) work out the real cost of a purchase; 3) choose between a market order and a limit order.

## Aula 3.1 — Authorized platform and KYC (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Your first crypto purchase might ask for your ID card, a selfie and your tax number. Strange? It isn't. It's there for a reason."

**[CENA 1]** A phone screen with a gold button that says "buy". Above it, a red warning: "Confirm before you continue."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before you buy anything, you need a platform. It's called an exchange, or trading platform. It's where you swap euros for cryptocurrencies and back again. And at this stage, the choice is the most important decision you'll make in the whole bootcamp. More important than the price, the coin or the timing. Why do I say that? Because most of the serious losses people suffer don't start with the market. They start with the platform.

**[CENA 2]** An illustration of a bank vault with a half-open door and a small warning sign beside it.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A platform that isn't authorized can run off with your money, freeze your account or lie about where the funds are. It doesn't have to be a sophisticated fraud. It just has to have nobody supervising what it does. Think of it this way: a restaurant with a license from the city council and one without a license can serve the same food. Only one of them has someone answerable to the law if something goes wrong. So the first test is simple: is the platform authorized, and does an official body supervise it? If the answer is no, the conversation ends here.

**[CENA 3]** A regulation document with the acronym "MiCA" highlighted, and beside it an official seal with the word "register".
**NARRAÇÃO:** In the European Union there is the MiCA regulation, which we've already talked about. Platforms that provide crypto-asset services need authorization and are supervised. In Portugal, the body that supervises the securities markets is the CMVM, the Portuguese securities market regulator. Go to the official website of that body, or of the authority in the country where the platform says it's based, and check whether the platform appears on the register of authorized providers. If it isn't there, it doesn't matter how nice the website looks.

**[CENA 4]** Three sources of information side by side: an advert with stars, an influencer video, an official register page. The first two are blurred; the third is sharp.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Don't rely only on a logo, an advert or an influencer who gets paid to talk about a platform. Trust the official register. Write down the platform's exact name, search for it in the register, and compare the name and the website domain with what's listed there. It sounds like extra work, and it's exactly the work that scammers hope you won't do. One minute of checking is worth more than any promotion that reaches you by message.

**[CENA 5]** A form screen with fields to fill in: name, date of birth, tax number, and a button to upload a photo of your document.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now, KYC. The letters stand for "know your customer", and it's the identity verification process. You'll need to provide an identity document, often a selfie holding that document, and your tax number. It might feel intrusive. But it's exactly what the law requires to fight money laundering and fraud. It's also the difference between a serious platform and one that just wants your money with no questions asked. Anyone who asks you for documents is doing their duty. Anyone who asks for nothing deserves a second look. Notice that this isn't distrust of you. Banks do exactly the same when you open an account, and nobody finds that strange. In crypto, a lot of people still think anonymity is a feature. Almost always, it's a risk signal.

**[CENA 6]** A phone receiving a message with a link. Below it, an alarm icon and the sentence: "Deposit now to unlock your account."
**NARRAÇÃO:** It's worth spotting a warning sign. If a platform lets you deposit without verifying your identity, be wary. If it asks you for money off the books, or to unlock an account, or to pay a fee you've never seen before, set it aside. KYC isn't an obstacle. It's a sign that someone is doing their homework. And a message rushing you to deposit is almost always a message from someone who wants your money before you've had time to think.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative case: Gonçalo, thirty-two, a maintenance technician in Setúbal, looking at a laptop with the official register open.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative case: Gonçalo, thirty-two, a maintenance technician in Setúbal, was about to deposit money into a platform that had reached him through a message. It looked well made, and it even had a support contact with a name. Before going ahead, he checked the official register and couldn't find the platform. He didn't buy anything. He learned that the message was a trap. It sounds like a small thing, but it was the most valuable decision he made that month.

**Texto no ecrã:** authorization · official register · KYC · warning sign
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Four-step exercise: write down the name of the platform you're considering, search for it in the official register, confirm the exact name and the website domain, and write in one line whether it's listed or not. Check question: why is an authorized platform more important than a good purchase price?

## Aula 3.2 — The real cost of buying (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "You buy a hundred euros of a crypto and, a minute later, it's worth less than a hundred euros. The price hasn't even moved. So what happened?"

**[CENA 1]** An animated purchase receipt. Each line appears in turn: "amount", "fee", "spread", "total". The total is underlined.
**NARRAÇÃO:** When you buy crypto, you pay more than the price you see on the screen. That's not a trick. These are costs, and it's worth knowing them before you buy, not after. Imagine you're buying an item of clothing in a shop, and at the till an extra amount appears that wasn't on the price tag. You don't feel cheated if you already knew that amount existed. You feel cheated when nobody warned you. There are three costs that almost everyone runs into. The first is the platform fee. It's a percentage of the value of the transaction, and it can vary depending on the payment method and the type of account. Always read the full fee table, not just the summary on the front page.

**[CENA 2]** A bar with two prices: on the left, "buy price", a little higher; on the right, "sell price", a little lower. A gap between the two bars, marked in gold.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The second cost is the spread. It's the difference between the price at which you buy and the price at which you sell, at the same moment. If the buy price is a bit higher than the sell price, that difference is the spread, and it stays with the platform or with whoever is making the market. It sounds like a detail, but you feel it on every trade, even when you don't notice it.

**[CENA 3]** A wallet icon receiving an arrow, labelled "network fee". The arrow changes size depending on demand.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The third cost, if you transfer to your own wallet, is the network fee. Every transfer on the blockchain has a cost, and it changes with how busy the network is. We've already seen this with Ethereum. It isn't the platform that charges it, it's the network itself. And anyone who doesn't know this is surprised when the amount that arrives is smaller than the amount they sent.

**[CENA 4]** Illustrative example: a paper receipt with round numbers. "€100 purchase", "− €1 fee", "− €0.50 spread", "= €98.50 real value".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's do the sums with round, hypothetical numbers. Illustrative example. You buy one hundred euros. The platform charges a one percent fee. The spread, at that moment, is half a percent. You're paying around one and a half euros in costs. About ninety-eight euros and fifty cents is left, in market terms. That seems small. But look at it from the other side. A cost of one and a half euros on a hundred-euro purchase looks like rounding. Only, the cost doesn't sit still: it works against you from the first second.

**[CENA 5]** An arrow pointing down two percent from the 98.50 euro position. The balance drops to 96.53, and a second arrow, pointing up, tries to get back to the starting point.
**NARRAÇÃO:** If the price falls two percent straight after, your balance drops to around ninety-six euros and fifty cents. You need the price to rise by more than two percent just to get back to where you started. The costs push you backwards before the market gives you any gain. And the more often you buy and sell, the more the costs eat into the result. Every trade carries a small, invisible tax, and it adds up. This isn't a rule made up by sellers. It's arithmetic. Someone who gets in and out often has to earn more just to stay where they were. This example isn't special in any way: it's the same for anyone buying the same amount, with the same fees.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben holding a calculator in his hand, turning the camera to show the screen.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is the part a lot of people skip. Think about the total cost of getting in and getting out. If you buy and sell, you pay twice. If you just hold, you pay once, but waiting also costs something, in risk and in opportunity. I'm not saying buying is bad. I'm saying the cost has to be on the sheet before the first click. If you only think about the price, you're seeing half the bill. And there's one detail that's worth its weight in gold: the cost of buying with a card is often different from the cost of transferring from a bank. Always ask which method is cheapest for your case, and compare against the official fee table, not against your memory.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative case: Inês comparing an app with a notebook, a pencil crossing out a line.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative case: Inês made a small purchase and noticed the app showed a slightly lower value than what she had paid. She thought the app was wrong. It wasn't. It was the fees and the spread. After working it out with pen and paper, she understood everything, and from then on she looked at the cost before every trade. It was the best way for her to learn, because the mistake cost her very little.

**Texto no ecrã:** platform fee · spread · network fee · cost of getting in and out
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise: write the three fees on a sheet of paper and work out a fifty-euro purchase using invented, round numbers. What is your position worth straight afterwards? Check question: if you pay fees on the way in and on the way out, how far does the market have to rise just to cover the costs?

## Aula 3.3 — Market order or limit order (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "You press the buy button, and the price on the screen isn't the price you pay. Let's understand why."

**[CENA 1]** Two buttons side by side: "market" and "limit". Under each, a short text explaining the difference.
**NARRAÇÃO:** When you place an order on a platform, you have to choose an order type. The two most common are the market order and the limit order. They sound like technical jargon, but they decide what happens to your money. Let's take them one at a time, calmly, because this is one of the points where people most often slip up on their very first purchase.

**[CENA 2]** An animation of an arrow shooting up fast, with a market order executed a little above the price shown on the screen.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A market order tells the platform: buy now, at whatever price is available. It's fast, and it guarantees the purchase goes through. The risk is that the price can move between the moment you press the button and the moment the order is executed. In fast markets, that's called slippage, and it can be significant. You're certain to buy, but you're not certain of the price. Think of a market stall: you pay the price written on the sign. On a busy platform, the price can change between when you press the button and when the order arrives. It's the same idea, just at a different speed.

**[CENA 3]** A horizontal line marked "€100". A limit order sits below it, labelled "I only buy up to here". The price arrow comes down, touches the line, and the order executes.
**NARRAÇÃO:** A limit order says: only buy if the price is equal to or lower than this value that I choose. You control the maximum price you'll pay. The risk is that the order never gets filled, if the price never reaches your level. You're left without a purchase, and nothing happens. It seems weak, but it's a form of protection. In practice, a limit order won't buy for you at the worst moment. It can also be only partly filled if there isn't enough liquidity at that price. Read the platform's terms on this point, because they vary from place to place.

**[CENA 4]** Illustrative example: an asset at €100. Two scenarios in parallel: one rises to €102, the other stays put, waiting.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's go with an illustrative example and invented numbers. Imagine an asset that's trading at one hundred euros, and you want to buy one hundred euros' worth of exposure. With a market order, you buy straight away, and the price may rise to one hundred and two in the second it takes to execute. With a limit order at ninety-eight, you only buy if the price falls to that level. If it doesn't fall, you don't buy. Neither one is the universal answer. Each one chooses a different risk.

**[CENA 5]** A set of scales with two pans: one says "certainty of execution", the other "certainty of price". The scales wobble, and the needle settles in the middle.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice what this means. A market order trades certainty of price for certainty of execution. A limit order trades certainty of execution for certainty of price. There isn't one that's better in every situation. Anyone who says one order type is always superior to the other is oversimplifying, and in markets, oversimplifying tends to be expensive. There's one that suits what you're doing, the time you have and how much price movement bothers you. Think about time too: if an order stays open for days, the market can change completely, and the order you wrote yesterday may no longer reflect what you want today.

**[CENA 6]** A table with two columns: "I use market when" and "I use limit when". Ruben pointing at the columns.
**NARRAÇÃO:** For someone just starting out, the practical rule is this. Use a limit order when you're not in a hurry and want to control the price. Use a market order when you need to get in now and you accept the price movement. But never use a market order in a moment of panic, because that's when the price moves the most. If the screen is red and you feel the urge to get in right now, that urge is your signal to stop and read your notes. And watch out for a common mistake: setting a limit too close to the current price, thinking it's a bargain, and then staring at an order that never fills. A limit has to be a decision, not a guess.

**[CENA 7]** Illustrative case: Gonçalo, the technician from Setúbal, looking at a trade history with a market order highlighted.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative case: Gonçalo placed a market order on a day of fast-moving news, and paid more than he had expected. It wasn't a disaster, but he learned that an order is a decision, not an automatic click. Since then, he always writes down the maximum price he's willing to pay before pressing any button.

**Texto no ecrã:** market order · limit order · slippage · execution vs price
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise: open the platform you've chosen, or a simulator, and watch the difference between the two order types. Don't buy anything for this. If the platform doesn't have a simulator, use a sheet of paper and a table with invented prices. Write how much you would pay with each one if the price moved two percent. Check question: when does a limit order leave you with nothing, and is that a mistake or a protection?

## Aula 3.4 — Your first euro (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Your first purchase should be the smallest of all. It isn't timidity. It's method."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben writing a number on a sheet of paper, then underlining it twice. A caption appears: "loss limit".
**NARRAÇÃO:** We've reached the moment of buying. Before that, I want you to set a limit. Not a profit target, a loss limit. An amount that, if it disappears, won't change your life. Choose it calmly, without rushing. Ask yourself: how much money can I lose without hurting my income, my food, the household bills, my emergency fund? That amount, or a small fraction of it, is your first euro. If you can't find an amount like that, don't buy. There's no hurry, and no coin will vanish because you waited a week. A loss limit isn't a forecast. It's a rule you write for yourself, before the market starts talking.

**[CENA 2]** An animated list of steps, each with a tick appearing: "2FA", "address verified", "small test", "decision made", "record kept".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's go through the practical steps, so you don't improvise. One: turn on two-factor authentication on the platform. It's the code that goes to your phone when you log in. Without it, anyone who finds out your password can get into your account. Two: confirm the address of everything you're going to use. The site, the app, the platform's name. Type the address by hand or save it in your bookmarks, and never click on links that reach you by message.

**[CENA 3]** A small deposit animation, with a symbolic amount going into the account and a tick appearing beside it.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Three: do a test run first. A small deposit, a small purchase and, if relevant, a small transfer to check the route works. Only then do you increase. The first operation is there to learn the process, not to make money. If something doesn't add up, you'll find out with little at stake. And a golden rule: don't test with the amount you intend to use for the big purchase. The test is for learning the route, not for testing your luck.

**[CENA 4]** A market screen flashing green, and below it a buy button with a small padlock showing it isn't loaded yet.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Four: buy only after you've made the decision, not during a price move. When the screen is flashing green, the temptation is to jump in now. That urge is the first sign to stop. Decisions made under the pressure of a chart are rarely the best, because the chart knows nothing about your life. A useful trick: write the decision down in a note before you open the app, with the amount and the reason. If the urge to buy passes, the note is still there, and you decide with a cool head.

**[CENA 5]** An open notebook with a table drawn in it: "date", "amount", "price", "fee", "order type", "reason".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Five: record everything. Date, amount, price, fee, order type, reason. It sounds like paperwork, but that's where you learn. In three months' time, you'll look back at this record and understand better than you do today what worked and what didn't. People who don't keep records tend to remember only what went well, and that fools anyone. If, when you review the record, you notice you buy whenever the price goes up and make mistakes when it goes down, you already have precious information about yourself. That's worth more than any technical analysis.

**[CENA 6]** Illustrative case: Inês with the printed checklist on a café table, each line ticked off in pen.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Illustrative case: Inês did exactly this. She chose an amount she didn't need, turned on authentication, tested with a small deposit and wrote each step in a notebook. She didn't get rich or poor in the first month, and that wasn't the goal anyway. She ended up with a process, and that's what we want you to have. The process is what protects you when emotions get in the way. And if one day the checklist tells you no, that isn't a failure. It's the process working.

**[CENA 7]** Ruben standing, medium shot, closing the notebook. On-screen text: "Educational content, not financial advice. The risk of loss is real."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Exercise: create a five-line checklist with the steps I've just gone through, and tick each one off as you do it. Don't move on to buying until you have all five. If you think one line doesn't apply, write down why, and talk to someone you trust before you cross it off. Let me repeat the essentials of this module: educational content, not financial advice, and the risk of loss is real. If the amount you've chosen doesn't let you sleep soundly, it's because it's too high.

**Texto no ecrã:** loss limit · 2FA · small test · record · educational content, not financial advice
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** Exercise: create the five-line checklist and tick off each step. Check question: what is the amount you're willing to lose without it changing your life, and why did you choose it?

**Missão do módulo 3:** write down, in writing, the maximum amount you can lose on this first step and the date on which you'll review the decision. Keep that sheet next to the checklist. No buying until you have both.

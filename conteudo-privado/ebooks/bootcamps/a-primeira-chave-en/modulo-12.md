Being cheap or expensive says nothing about direction. It can get even cheaper tomorrow. The trend is the only thing that tells you which way the market is pushing.

But the second is the kind of entry the method looks for, because the wind is blowing in the same direction as the boat. That is the difference we want you to train. Look at this carefully, because it is the basis of everything that follows.

## Aula 12.2 — The three-DEMA filter and the momentum confirmation (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Saying 'there is a trend' is easy. Proving it with numbers is another story. Three averages and a strength indicator are there for that."

**[CENA 1]** One-hour chart with three lines: DEMA 15 in light gold, DEMA 50 in gold, DEMA 238 in grey.
**NARRAÇÃO:** To measure the trend, Aurum Flow uses three DEMA averages, of 15, 50 and 238 periods. A DEMA lags less than a simple average and reacts faster. The 15 shows the recent move, the 50 the medium term, the 238 the background. When all three are ordered in the same direction and the price is on the same side, the filter says there is a trend. When they are tangled together, the filter says there isn't. And a filter that says "no" is as useful as one that says "yes", because it saves you a bad entry.

**[CENA 2]** Two charts: on the left, the three lines ordered and the price above them; on the right, the lines crossed. Captions: "aligned" · "tangled".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice the difference. On the left, the three averages are ordered, with the fastest on top and the slowest at the bottom, and the price is trading above them. That is an uptrend. On the right, the lines cross each other and the price keeps crossing them from one side to the other. That is sideways. Both are illustrative examples. If you look at ten charts like these, you will see that sideways shows up more often than you would like. With the filter, it is a "no" without drama, not a signal you have to force.

**[CENA 3]** Ruben with a finger on the screen, pointing at the RSI indicator in the lower panel. Text: "RSI · momentum confirmation".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The trend filter tells you which way to look, but not when. That is where the momentum confirmation comes in, done with the RSI. The RSI measures the recent strength of moves on a scale from zero to one hundred. On a buy entry, we want the RSI to confirm strength in the same direction as the signal, not turn against it. On a sell entry, it is the opposite. There is no magic number here for you to memorize. The rule is the direction: momentum has to be on the side of the trend.

**[CENA 4]** Golden checklist on screen with two boxes: "trend: three DEMA aligned" and "momentum: RSI confirming". Both ticked.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Put the two parts together and you have the entry gate. First, the three DEMA say there is a trend and say which way it goes. Second, the RSI confirms that the strength is going in the same direction. Only with both, the signal is eligible. If one fails, there is no signal. That is not a preference, it is a closed door. And notice that this does not oblige you to pick the top or the bottom. It obliges you to wait for the market to show its hand before you show yours. Don't jump to conclusions from what you have just heard.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben writing three questions in his notebook: "DEMA aligned? · RSI confirming? · same direction?"
**NARRAÇÃO:** Do the exercise you will repeat in every lesson of this module. Choose a one-hour chart. First question: are the three averages ordered? Second: does the RSI confirm the same direction? Third: do both answers point to the same side? If the answer to any of them is "no", write "no signal" and move on to the next chart. It is not a failure. It is the filter doing its job, and doing it without asking you for an opinion. There is no shortcut here, and that is why it is worth practising. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot, charcoal background. Line on screen: "The filter doesn't predict. Ask the same question every time."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. The three DEMA answer the trend question. The RSI answers the momentum question. The signal only exists when both answers point the same way. There is no prediction here. There is a question asked the same way every time, so that you don't decide based on your mood of the day. In the next lesson we read a complete signal, from entry to the last target, and see the percentage distances that make it comparable. Pay close attention to this, because it is the basis of everything that follows. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

## Aula 12.3 — Reading a signal: entry, stop and % distances (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "A signal is a card with four numbers. If you can read all four, you know what you are accepting before you accept it."

**[CENA 1]** Aurum Flow signal screen, zoomed in on the card. Captions: "entry" · "stop" · "targets". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is a signal. It is an illustrative example, put together for the lesson. At first glance four things matter: the entry price, the stop, the three targets, and the distance of each level in points and in percentage. The signal is a static plan. It was fixed on the candle that generated it and it does not rewrite itself when the price moves. If the price has already moved away from the entry, the plan stays exactly the same. This distinction will matter in the mission, because you will compare the written plan with what the market did afterwards.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: a vertical line with the entry, the stop below it and a bracket marked "1.5 × ATR". Text: "stop = 1.5 × ATR".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The stop sits at a distance of one and a half ATR. ATR is the average range of recent candles. It tells you how much the asset usually moves in one hourly candle. By multiplying by one and a half, the stop is far enough that normal noise does not hit it, and close enough that a loss is the expected one. The idea is for the stop to follow the asset's real behavior, not a fixed number you chose on impulse. A stop chosen on impulse is a bet in disguise.

**[CENA 3]** Ruben with a calculator. On screen: "entry 100 · stop −2%". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's do the maths, with an illustrative example. The entry is one hundred, and the stop sits two per cent below, that is, at ninety-eight. That is your risk distance: two per cent. Now the targets. The first, at one and a half to one, is three per cent above the entry. The second, at three to one, six per cent. The third, at six to one, twelve per cent. Notice that every number is measured against the risk, never against the absolute price. That is what lets you compare signals on assets with completely different prices.

**[CENA 4]** Table on screen: "Entry 100 · Stop 98 (−2%) · Target 1: 103 (+3%) · Target 2: 106 (+6%) · Target 3: 112 (+12%)". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Here is the same example in a table. Entry one hundred, stop ninety-eight, and the targets at one hundred and three, one hundred and six and one hundred and twelve. Again: illustrative example, because a number without context invites fantasy. I am not telling you the price will get there. I am telling you that, if you enter, this is what the plan asks for. Each level costs the two per cent risk and returns one and a half, three or six times that risk, if it is reached. Nothing more, and nothing less. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben at the notebook, with a form: "entry · stop · stop distance in % · target 1 · target 2 · target 3".
**NARRAÇÃO:** To practise, copy this form into your notebook and always fill it in the same order. Entry, stop, the stop distance in percentage, and the three targets with their respective distances. If a signal does not let you fill in these fields without guessing, it is not complete enough for you yet. Don't use it. Read the card until you can fill it in from memory, without help, and only then move on to the decision. The order matters: first you read, then you decide. Pay close attention to this, because it is the basis of everything that follows. There is no shortcut here, and that is why it is worth practising.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot. Line on screen: "Read before you act."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. A signal is a fixed plan with four parts. The entry is where the plan starts. The stop is where the plan admits it was wrong, measured in ATR. The targets are where the plan collects, measured in multiples of the risk. The percentage distances let you compare signals and understand the weight of each one on your balance. In the next lesson we talk about the targets in more detail, and about a rule you have to write down before you need it: what you do when the first target is hit. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

## Aula 12.4 — The three targets, partial exits and break-even (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "When the price goes your way, the problem changes. It is no longer what you do if you are wrong. It is what you do when you are right."

**[CENA 1]** Chart with the entry and the three targets as horizontal gold lines, of increasing intensity. Text: "1:1.5 · 1:3 · 1:6".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The three targets sit at one and a half, three and six times the risk. They are not three predictions. They are three decision points. The first is the nearest, so it is the one reached first. The last is the most ambitious, and it may never be reached. A signal that reaches the second target did not fail at the first. Each target is a different moment, with its own decision, and that is why the plan separates them instead of lumping them into a single number. Don't jump to conclusions from what you have just heard.

**[CENA 2]** Ruben showing his notebook, on the page of exit rules. Text: "partial exit · written before entry".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now the part that changes how you trade: partial exits. The idea is simple. You don't have to close the whole position in the same place. You can close one part at the first target, another at the second, and leave a part running for the third. What you have already collected stays collected, and what remains has room to run. But the percentage of each part is not a number you pick on top of the chart. It is a rule you write before entering. Decided during the trade, it is already emotion, and emotion tends to choose the wrong exit.

**[CENA 3]** Screen with the stop moving up to the entry after the first target. Caption: "break-even: stop at entry". Text: "personal rule · written beforehand".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Break-even is the step most people skip and later regret. When the price hits the first target, you can move the stop to the entry. If the market comes back, the part still open closes near zero, instead of giving back the full risk. Careful: this is your rule. Don't count on any button doing it for you, and the decision to apply it is entirely yours. Write it in the words you will use. For example: when the first target is hit, I close one part and move the stop to the entry.

**[CENA 4]** Example chart: the price rises to target 1, then returns to the entry. Label: illustrative example · no guarantees.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's look at this with an illustrative example, with the entry at one hundred and the stop at ninety-eight. The price rises to one hundred and three. Say you close one part and move the stop to one hundred. Then the market turns back and reaches one hundred. The part still open closes at break-even, and the part you collected at the first target stays as profit. If the price had dropped right after the entry, the loss would be the original stop, and nothing more. What the rule changes is what happens after the plan has proven it was right.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben in a close-up, speaking to the camera. Text: "Don't copy anyone's percentages."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Now the notebook exercise. Write four lines: the percentage you close at the first target, the one you close at the second, the part you leave running for the third, and the stop rule. You don't need to copy anyone's percentages, not even the ones that appear in this course. You need to have your own. And also write down what you will do if the price reaches the second target and turns back. Whoever doesn't have that line written always decides at the last minute, and at the last minute nobody decides well. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot. On-screen text: "1:1.5 · 1:3 · 1:6 · exits written · break-even is your rule".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. Three targets, three decisions. Partial exits that you define before entering. Break-even as a written rule, which protects the trade once the plan has been right. None of this guarantees a profit. It is a way of not letting emotion decide what you do with an open trade. In the next lesson, the part that protects the most: when not to enter. Most of a beginner's mistakes do not happen inside the trade. They happen before it, when they enter without a reason. Pay close attention to this, because it is the basis of everything that follows. There is no shortcut here, and that is why it is worth practising.

**Texto no ecrã:** 1:1.5 · 1:3 · 1:6 · partial exit written · break-even is your rule

## Aula 12.5 — When not to enter, and the mission (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "The lesson fewest people want to see is the one that protects the most. In this method, the best decision is often not to enter."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben in a medium shot, almost black background. On-screen text: "Not entering is also a decision."
**NARRAÇÃO:** There are three situations in which the rule is not to enter, even when a signal is on the screen. The first: the signal goes against the trend. We have already seen why. If the three DEMA say one thing and the signal says another, there is no signal for you, however good it looks. The second: there is a high-impact news event scheduled for the next few hours. The third: you have already reached your daily loss limit. That is the most important one, and we will look at it calmly in module thirteen, because that is where many accounts are lost.

**[CENA 2]** Chart with a sell signal marked in red. Beside it, the three DEMA aligned upwards. Text: "against the trend → don't enter". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Look at this illustrative example. A sell signal appears, but the three averages are aligned upwards. There is a pullback candle, and instinct says "now". It isn't. The trend is on the other side, and the signal is just a reaction to a pause. When in doubt, ask yourself just one thing: am I entering with the trend? If the answer is no, the answer to the entry is also no. You don't need more arguments than that. Don't jump to conclusions from what you have just heard. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

**[CENA 3]** Economic calendar with a day marked in gold. Text: "high-impact news · no new entries in that window".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The second situation is the calendar. Before each session, check whether there is a high-impact news event scheduled for the next few hours. If there is, the rule is simple: you do not open new positions in that window. In those hours, the price can jump for reasons no one-hour chart can read. You can keep studying, marking and recording. What you don't do is enter on top of news you cannot control, just because the chart looked clean five minutes earlier. Pay close attention to this, because it is the basis of everything that follows.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben with his notebook. Three boxes below the exit rules: "against the trend", "strong news", "daily limit reached".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Write these three no-entry rules below your exit rules. Against the trend: I don't enter. Strong news in the window: I don't open a new position. Daily limit reached: I don't enter any more that day, even if the signal looks perfect on paper. Notice that these rules do not depend on how you feel. They depend on facts that either hold or don't. That is what makes them useful, and what makes them easy to follow when you need them most. There is no shortcut here, and that is why it is worth practising. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

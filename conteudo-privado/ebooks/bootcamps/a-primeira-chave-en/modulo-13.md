# Módulo 13 — Risk and position size (≈15 min)
**Objetivo:** make sure no single trade can ruin the account, sizing each one from the stop distance and never from the wish to win.  **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) set the maximum risk per trade at 1% of the balance; 2) calculate the size of a position from the stop distance; 3) read the useful leverage and the Aurum Flow account panel; 4) set a daily limit and stopping rules for losing streaks.

**Nota de produção:** use a €1,000 simulated account balance throughout the module, always with "illustrative example" on screen and in voice. The Aurum Flow panel is recorded with the day's data, with the values blurred.

## Aula 13.1 — 1% risk per trade (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "You can have the best strategy in the world and still end up with no account. A run of big, bad trades is all it takes. The defence is not to be right more often. It is to lose a little at a time."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben in a medium shot, almost black background. On-screen text: "Educational content. The risk of loss is real."
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is the most tedious module of level three and, for many people, the most important. Before we talk numbers, the usual warning. Educational content. The risk of loss is real, and it can be total. This lesson does not promise you will win. It promises you something more modest and more useful: that one bad trade will not take away what it took you years to build. That is the only promise a course on risk can honestly make. And even that depends on you, on following the rule every day, with no exceptions that seem reasonable at the time.

**[CENA 2]** Animation: a balance bar divided into a hundred equal slices, with one gold slice lit. Text: "1% of the balance per trade".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The rule we are going to use is this: you risk at most one per cent of the balance on each trade. One per cent, not whatever you think the signal deserves. If you have €1,000 in your simulated account, the maximum risk of a trade is €10. And be careful: risk is not the amount invested. It is how much you lose if the stop is hit. They are different things, and confusing the two is the origin of many accounts that disappear. Notice that the gold slice is small on purpose. A small slice does not bring you down. Illustrative example, with a simulated account, as always.

**[CENA 3]** Two blocks side by side. Left: "invested €500 · stop −2% · risk €10". Right: "invested €200 · stop −15% · risk €30". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Look at these two cases, illustrative examples. In the first, you invest five hundred euros, but the stop is at two per cent. If it is hit, you lose ten euros. In the second, you invest only two hundred, but the stop is at fifteen per cent, and you lose thirty. Anyone who only looks at the amount invested thinks the first is the big risk. They are wrong. The number that controls the account is the loss at the stop. So the right question is not "how much do I invest?", it is "how much do I lose if the stop is hit?". That question protects the balance, and it is the one we will train throughout the module.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben with an open notebook. On the page, the formula: "maximum risk = balance × 1%". Below: "€1,000 × 1% = €10".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Write the formula at the top of your notebook: balance times one per cent. With a thousand simulated euros, the loss ceiling of each trade is ten euros. That ceiling does not change because the signal looks good to you. An exciting signal does not entitle you to risk more. A weak signal may lead you to risk less, and that is also fine. What changes from trade to trade is the size of the position, and that is next lesson. The risk stays fixed. If you ever write another number in your notebook, stop and go back to this page. The mistake is in the calculation, never in the market.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben in a close-up, speaking to the camera. Text: "ten losses in a row of 1% ≈ 10% of the account".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's be realistic. You will lose trades. That is part of the method and not a sign that the method is broken. With one per cent, a loss is a scratch. Ten losses in a row of one per cent each take roughly ten per cent off the account. It is a bad stretch, and it recovers with patience and rules. What destroys accounts is the opposite: risking ten or twenty per cent on one trade because this time is different. If you ever hear that sentence in your head, remember the formula. It does not argue. It calculates. And that is why it works.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot, charcoal background. List on screen: "1% of the balance · risk is the loss at the stop · €1,000 → €10".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. The risk of each trade is one per cent of the balance. It is not what you invest, it is what you lose if the stop is hit. This rule protects you from streaks, because no single loss can take you out of the game. It also forces you to do something DCA has already taught you: decide the size before you feel the pressure of the market. In the next lesson we take this rule and calculate the exact size of the position, from the stop distance you learned in module twelve. It is not magic. It is a division.

**Texto no ecrã:** 1% of the balance · risk = loss at the stop · €1,000 → €10
**Check question:** You have €1,000 in a simulated account. What is the maximum risk on one trade? Answer: €10, one per cent. Illustrative example.

## Aula 13.2 — Calculating the size from the stop distance (≈3–4 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "You already know how much you can lose: ten euros. The question almost nobody asks is still missing: how much can I buy so that, if the stop is hit, I lose exactly those ten?"

**[CENA 1]** Screen with the module 12 example: entry at 100, stop at 98. Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's use the module twelve example, with the entry at one hundred and the stop two per cent below, at ninety-eight. Illustrative example, as always. The simulated account has a thousand euros and the maximum risk is ten. The question is: how much should I have invested in this position so that the stop, if hit, costs exactly ten euros? It is not a number you guess. It has a formula, and the formula fits on a card. I will show it to you slowly, with the calculator in hand, so that you can redo the calculation yourself without looking at me.

**[CENA 2]** Diagram with the formula highlighted: "Size = risk in € ÷ stop distance in %".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The formula is this: the size of the position equals the risk in euros divided by the stop distance in percentage. The risk is ten euros. The stop distance is two per cent. Ten divided by two per cent, or by zero point zero two in the calculator, gives five hundred. Five hundred euros of position. If the price falls two per cent, you lose two per cent of five hundred, which is ten euros. Exactly your risk, not a cent more. Each piece has a role: the risk sets the ceiling, the stop sets the distance, and the size is what fits between the two.

**[CENA 3]** Table on screen: "stop 2% → position €500 · stop 4% → €250 · stop 5% → €200". Text: "same risk, different sizes".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice what happens when you change only the stop distance, keeping the risk at ten euros. With the stop at two per cent, the position is five hundred euros. With the stop at four per cent, it drops to two hundred and fifty. With the stop at five per cent, it is two hundred. The risk is always ten. A wider stop forces a smaller position. A tighter stop lets you buy more. Illustrative example, but the lesson is firm: never choose the size before you know where the stop is. The stop decides the position, not the other way round.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben at the notebook, with four numbered steps: "1 balance · 2 risk of 1% · 3 stop distance in % · 4 size = 2 ÷ 3".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Write the procedure in four steps, always in the same order. One: the account balance. Two: the risk, one per cent of that balance. Three: the stop distance in percentage, taken from the signal plan. Four: you divide two by three. That result is the size. If you don't have the stop distance, you don't have a signal, and no size can be calculated. Don't guess the distance or round it to whatever suits you. It comes from the plan written before you entered, and that is where it should stay.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben with a second example in the notebook: "€10 ÷ 3% = €333 → rounded down to €330". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Second example, also illustrative. The stop is now at three per cent. Ten divided by zero point zero three gives three hundred and thirty-three. You don't round up. Rounding up is increasing the risk without meaning to, and an account that grows without you noticing is exactly what we want to avoid. You round down, to three hundred and thirty, without hesitating. Notice that the wider stop gave a smaller position, as we saw a moment ago. The rule always adds up, and that is what makes it reliable. When it doesn't, the mistake is in the calculation.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot. On-screen line: "Stop first, size after."
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. Stop first, size after. The formula is the risk in euros divided by the stop distance in percentage, and you always round down. There is no opinion in this calculation. There is a distance, a risk and a result. If one day you feel the size "should" be bigger because the signal is good, remember this lesson: conviction is not part of the formula. In the next lesson we look at leverage, which many people confuse with size, and at the Aurum Flow account panel, which shows several of these calculations already done.

**Texto no ecrã:** size = risk € ÷ stop distance % · round down · stop first
**Check question:** Risk of €10, stop at 5%. What is the size? Answer: €200. Illustrative example.
**Fecho / CTA da aula:** "Next lesson: useful leverage and the account panel."

## Aula 13.3 — Useful leverage and the account panel (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "Leverage is the button most people confuse with position size. And confusing the two is the shortest route to losing more than you wanted."

**[CENA 1]** Screen with a leverage control being moved. Text: "leverage ≠ size ≠ risk".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's separate three things that look like one. Leverage is the multiplier the platform gives you to open a position bigger than the money you have deposited. Size, or notional, is the total value of the position. And risk is how much you lose if the stop is hit. Leverage does not create risk out of nothing. It changes how much money you have to set aside as margin, and it makes it easy to open large positions without noticing. If the size and the stop have not changed, the risk has not changed either, whatever leverage you chose.

**[CENA 2]** Aurum Flow account panel, with today's values blurred. Text: "useful leverage · notional · fees · risk % per trade".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The Aurum Flow account panel shows four things for the size you chose. The useful leverage: the leverage the position needs for the size you chose, not the maximum the platform offers. The notional, which is the total value of the position. The cost in fees, on entry and exit, which eats part of the result. And the risk as a percentage per trade. The values on screen are blurred, because they do not matter for the lesson. What matters is learning to read all four, not memorizing numbers.

**[CENA 3]** Ruben with the example on screen: "account €1,000 · position €500 · useful leverage ≈ 1×". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Apply this to the example from a moment ago: a thousand-euro account, a five-hundred-euro position. The useful leverage is low, about one times. That means the position fits in the account without stretching anything. Now imagine the panel showed you ten times for the same position. What would change? The size would still be five hundred, and the stop would still be at two per cent. The only thing that would change is the margin the platform asks to hold the position. Whoever chooses the leverage is not choosing the risk. The risk was already written into the stop and the formula.

**[CENA 4]** Ruben at the notebook, with the rule written in bold: "use the leverage the size requires, never the maximum".
**NARRAÇÃO:** The rule you write in your notebook is this, without exceptions: the leverage I use is the one the calculated size requires, never the maximum offered, even if the platform presents it as the normal option. If the account fits without leverage, I don't use leverage. If the position needs some leverage, the panel shows it to you. And you always check the notional before you press confirm. It is not a rule of fear, it is a rule of precision. The platform shows the maximum because that is what it shows. Your interest is different: it is the size you calculated in the previous lesson, and nothing else.

**[CENA 5]** Ruben with the fee calculation on screen: "illustrative example: 0.1% fee per side · position €500 → €0.50 on entry + €0.50 on exit".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Finally, fees. In an illustrative example, with a fee of zero point one per cent per side, a five-hundred-euro position pays fifty cents on entry and fifty cents on exit. It seems little. But with a two per cent stop and a risk of ten euros, one euro of fees is ten per cent of the risk you defined. It is not a detail. That is why the panel shows the cost, and why you look at it before you confirm, not after. The real fee is your platform's, and it is in its terms. Check it. Don't use mine as if it were yours.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a medium shot. On-screen text: "leverage ≠ size ≠ risk · 4 numbers on the panel · fees count".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Summary. Leverage is how the platform asks for margin. Size is the value of the position. Risk is the loss at the stop. Don't confuse the three. The Aurum Flow panel shows the useful leverage, the notional, the fees and the risk per trade, and your rule is to look at all four before pressing any button. In the last lesson of the module we talk about what happens after a loss, after a bad day, and how to stop in time, before a bad day turns into a bad week.

**Texto no ecrã:** leverage ≠ size ≠ risk · 4 numbers on the panel · fees count
**Check question:** A €500 position in a €1,000 account. Is the useful leverage high or low? Answer: low, about one times. The position fits in the account without stretching. Illustrative example.

## Aula 13.4 — Daily limit, losing streaks and stopping rules (≈3 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** "The worst decision in an account is rarely the first loss. It is the fourth, after a bad morning, when you decide that 'now is the time'."

**[CENA 1]** Ruben in a medium shot. On-screen text: "a bad day can also be planned for".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Last lesson of the module, and perhaps the one that saves you the most. Everything we have seen so far is about single trades. But whoever loses money almost always loses in sequence. One loss, then another, then a third, and the head starts asking for an immediate recovery. That urge has a name: chasing losses. And the antidote is not willpower, which runs out quickly. It is a rule written before the day starts. Whoever decides what to do after three losses at ten in the morning is already deciding with a head in revenge mode. That is why the decision has to be made beforehand.

**[CENA 2]** Bar chart with three red bars, each marked "−1%". Label: illustrative example.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Look at this series, illustrative example. Three losses of one per cent each. On the thousand-euro account, that is around thirty euros. It hurts, but it is not the end. The problem starts when the fourth trade comes out bigger, because the person wants to recover quickly. At that moment, the risk stops being the one per cent of the rule and becomes a desperate bet. Nobody decides that on purpose. It happens gradually, with the justification that "this time it pays off". The stopping rule exists to prevent that shift, and so you don't have to argue about it with yourself in the worst moment.

**[CENA 3]** Ruben with the notebook, writing three stopping rules. Text: "daily limit · losing streak · stop is stop".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Write three stopping rules, with your own numbers. The first is the daily loss limit. For example, and it is only an illustrative example, two per cent of the balance in one day: once you hit it, you close the platform. The second is the losing streak: three losses in a row on the same day, and you stop. The third is the most important: after a stop, you don't come back to the screen that day, not even to "see if the signal has improved". This is the rule that saves the most accounts, because the market owes you nothing and it will always be back tomorrow. The numbers you write are yours. The ones I use here are small on purpose.

**[CENA 4]** Two-minute checklist on screen: "limit written? · streak written? · maximum trades? · news in the window checked?".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before each session, run a two-minute routine. Is the daily limit written down? Is the losing streak written down? Do you know the maximum number of trades you will take? Have you checked the news in the window? If the answer to any of these is no, you don't start. Notice that this routine says nothing about the market. It says everything about you. It is the part of trading that most resembles the Friday ritual of the DCA: rules before the money, not during it. Someone who has this routine done does not need to be smarter than the market. They need to be more predictable than their own emotions.

**[CENA 5]** Screen with the text: "Module 13 mission: size calculator for 5 signals, in a €1,000 simulated account".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Module mission. Choose five past Aurum Flow signals and do the calculations in a simulated account of one thousand euros. For each signal, fill in the size calculator: the risk in euros, the stop distance in percentage, the calculated size rounded down, the useful leverage the panel shows, and the cost of fees. Don't open any real position for this. You need to do the calculation five times, without skipping any. If one of them leaves you in doubt, go back to the formula and not to instinct. The calculator has no opinion. Your intuition does, and that is why the calculator comes first.

**[CENA 6]** Ruben in a close-up, with the warning on screen: "Educational content, not financial advice. The risk of loss is real, and it can be total."
**NARRAÇÃO:** End of module thirteen, and of the risk block. One per cent per trade. Size from the stop. Leverage that the size requires, never the maximum. Daily limit and losing streak written down before the first signal. None of these rules guarantees a profit. What they do is limit what a bad streak can take from you, and that is already a lot. Educational content, not financial advice. The risk of loss is real, and it can be total.

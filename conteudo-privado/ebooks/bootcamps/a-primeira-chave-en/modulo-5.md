# Módulo 5 — Anti-scam shield (≈17 min)
**Objetivo:** recognize the most common crypto scams before you click, send or approve anything. **No fim, o aluno consegue:** 1) identify five scam patterns; 2) check a link, a support contact or an airdrop before acting; 3) apply the 10-point checklist.

## Aula 5.1 — Fake support and requests for your key (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** Imagine you get a message from a platform's support team saying your account has been blocked and you have to act right now. If that makes you nervous, this lesson is for you.

**[CENA 1]** A phone screen with a private message notification. The logo looks almost identical to the original, but the lettering is slightly different. A clock in the corner is counting down.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's start with the most common scam, and also the easiest one to stop. Someone messages you on social media or in a group chat and presents themselves as a platform's support team. They say your account has been blocked, that there's an urgent verification, and that they need to confirm something. The message looks real. It has the right logo, the right language and even a reference number. But it's a lie, and the urgency is the first clue. Whoever is trying to catch you needs you to make a quick mistake, without thinking twice. That's why the countdown clock isn't a detail. It's their main weapon.

**[CENA 2]** Three padlocks animated as they close, labelled: password, two-factor code, seed phrase.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Here's the rule that is never broken. No legitimate support team will ask you for your seed phrase. They won't ask for your password, or for the code that arrives by SMS or in the app. And they won't write to you first to say you have a problem. The seed phrase, those twelve or twenty-four words you keep to recover your wallet, is the key to everything you hold there. Whoever has it has the money. There are no exceptions, not for support, not for a technician who calls you, not for anyone who says they work with the platform. If a message asks you to confirm something you never even looked for, the answer is no.

**[CENA 3]** Illustrative case. A café in Coimbra, Inês sitting by the window with her phone in her hand. Small caption: "illustrative case".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let me tell you an illustrative case. Inês, twenty-nine, a nurse in Coimbra, received a message like this one on an afternoon off, while she was having a coffee. The support name looked official, and they asked her to validate her account through a website. She was suspicious for a simple reason: the message arrived without her having asked anyone for help. Instead of clicking, she closed the conversation, opened the app she already had installed and looked for herself. There was no block at all. There was a trap waiting for one tap.

**[CENA 4]** A split screen. On the left, a long, strange link. On the right, the official app, with the address typed by hand into the browser.
**NARRAÇÃO:** That's exactly what Inês did, and it's what you should do too. If you think there's a problem with an account, don't use the link you received. Open the official app, or type the website address by hand into your browser. Look for the information there. If the problem is real, you'll find it in the right place, in the security area or in the notifications. If nothing shows up, the message was fake, and you stopped it without losing anything. This habit seems small, but it's what separates people who lose money from people who don't. Also save the official address in your bookmarks, so you can use it next time without relying on anyone.

**[CENA 5]** Large text on screen: "If someone asks for your key, it's a scam." The word "support" is crossed out in red.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Keep this sentence: if someone asks for your key, it's a scam, even if they look like support. Don't reply out of politeness, don't send a test message and don't click to "sort it out quickly". Block the contact, report it on the platform where it appeared, and warn anyone who might be in the same situation, especially older family members, who are a favorite target for these messages. Scammers buy lists of accounts and phone numbers at random, and send thousands of identical messages. It isn't a personal attack, it's a volume business, which is why the best response is to give them nothing at all. If by any chance you've already given away your seed phrase, consider the wallet compromised. Create a new wallet on a clean device, and move what you can as quickly as possible. Then contact the platform. Every minute counts, because the person who scammed you is in a hurry too.

**[CENA 6]** The clock from scene 1 stops at zero. An arrow points to lesson 5.2.
**NARRAÇÃO:** To sum up: nobody legitimate asks for your key, urgency is always a clue, and the link in a message is never the right way in. In the next lesson we'll look at the promise to double your money. It seems very different, but it's the same story in different clothes, and the maths behind it is even more revealing.

**Texto no ecrã:** official support never asks for your key · urgency is an alarm · address typed by hand · if they ask for your key, it's a scam
**Pergunta de verificação:** If a support team asks you for your seed phrase, what do you do? Answer: nothing. You block them, report them and warn anyone who might be at risk.

## Aula 5.2 — Doubling, pyramids and promises (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** If someone guarantees that your money will double in a few days, the first thing you should feel is distrust, not excitement.

**[CENA 1]** An on-screen advert: "Send 500 and receive 1,000 in 7 days". Below it, a label: "guaranteed doubling".
**NARRAÇÃO:** This is the oldest promise in the world in new clothes. You send crypto to an address, or you join a group, and they tell you you'll get double back in a short time. Sometimes it's presented as a prize draw, sometimes as a sign-up bonus, sometimes as a rewards programme that levels up. The format changes, but the structure is almost always the same. Before anything else, ask yourself one very simple question: where does the money for that doubling come from? If there's no answer, the conversation ends there.

**[CENA 2]** An animated diagram: people joining from the bottom, money rising to those at the top, and more and more small circles coming together.
**NARRAÇÃO:** In a pyramid scheme, the money paid to the first participants doesn't come from any real product or activity. It comes from the people who join later. To pay those who joined early, more and more people have to keep joining. And here's the arithmetic that's usually hidden. Imagine that each person has to bring in two new people to move up a level. By the tenth level, that step alone needs one thousand and twenty-four new people. By the twentieth, it's more than a million. No market can sustain that sum for long, and whoever joins late is the one left paying.

**[CENA 3]** An animated list with four signs, one at a time: "fixed high return", "today only", "bring friends to level up", "fees to withdraw".
**NARRAÇÃO:** There are signs that keep repeating. They promise a fixed, high return, with no risk explained. They create time pressure, with phrases like "today only" or "last place". They pay bonuses for bringing in friends, rather than for using a product or a service. And when you ask to take money out, fees, deadlines or new requirements appear, as if you had to pay to get out. If you see two or more of these signs at the same time, it's not the moment to study the offer calmly. It's the moment to leave.

**[CENA 4]** Illustrative case. A small office in Leiria, Tiago, thirty-four, a refrigeration technician, looking at his phone. Caption: "illustrative case".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let me tell you an illustrative case. Tiago, thirty-four, a refrigeration technician in Leiria, joined a group because a friend told him he had got double his money back. At first, everything seemed to work, and they even paid him a small amount to build his trust. Then they asked him for another two hundred euros to "level up". When he tried to withdraw what he had, the request was "under review" for weeks. He realized too late that the payments he was receiving came from the money of the people who joined after him. As this is an illustrative case, the numbers belong to nobody, but the pattern repeats in many stories.

**[CENA 5]** A blank screen with one large question: "Who pays your profit?"
**NARRAÇÃO:** The question you should always ask is this: who pays the profit? In a real investment, the return comes from something real, such as an activity, a company or a market with genuine supply and demand. It can go up or down, and you can lose everything, but there's a visible source. If the answer is "the people who join next", you're looking at a pyramid, however well packaged it is. And pay attention to the pressure to invite other people, because that's how the scheme grows. When your success depends on bringing in people, the product is the entry itself.

**[CENA 6]** A split screen. On the left, a chart that always goes up. On the right, the same chart with a warning line saying "withdrawal blocked".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Notice a detail that fools a lot of people. The first payments are usually real. They're there precisely to give you confidence. The problem shows up when you want to withdraw a large amount, and that's when the door slowly closes. So never judge an offer by what you've already received. Judge it by how easily you're allowed to get out. If you can't get out easily, it isn't an investment, it's a trap with a nice-looking receipt. So what do you do when someone promises you double? You don't join, you don't send anything to test it, and you don't ask the contact who wrote to you for more details. Search for the project's name together with the word "scam" and read what other people have said. And before any money decision, apply the 24-hour rule, which we'll look at more closely in the checklist. An honest offer can handle that wait without any problem.

**Texto no ecrã:** guaranteed doubling = alarm · where does the money come from? · pyramid = profit depends on new entries · the exit tells you everything
**Pergunta de verificação:** If a project only pays bonuses for bringing in friends, where does the money for the bonuses come from? Answer: from the people who join later, not from a real product.

## Aula 5.3 — Phishing, airdrops and approvals (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** Sometimes a single swapped letter in a website address is enough to make you lose your entire wallet. Let's see how that happens.

**[CENA 1]** Two almost identical addresses side by side. One letter in the second address is highlighted in red.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let's talk about phishing. It's a copy. Scammers build a site that looks identical to the original, with the same logo, the same colors and the same buttons. Often only one letter in the address changes, or they use a lookalike domain with one extra little dash. When you get there, you type in your password or connect your wallet, and whoever set up the site keeps whatever you gave them. The site looks right, but the address is different. And the difference is in the address, which is what saves you. These sites are built in minutes and tend to last only briefly, because they're reported quickly. So don't trust the appearance, or how fast the page loads.

**[CENA 2]** A search engine screen with the first result marked "Ad".
**NARRAÇÃO:** There's an extra caution here. Sponsored ads at the top of search engines can also lead to fake sites. Don't trust a result just because it appears first. Look for the official site, check the address and, if you can, save it in your browser bookmarks. Notice that the genuine site rarely asks you for anything urgent on its homepage. If a page pressures you to enter details, be suspicious of the page itself.

**[CENA 3]** A message on screen: "You've received 5,000 free tokens! Connect your wallet to claim them". A red counter counting down.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Airdrops are free distributions of tokens. They do exist, but they're rare, and they almost never arrive by private message from a stranger. When someone offers you free tokens and asks you to connect your wallet to a site, you're giving a permission. And that permission can allow someone to move your funds. The red counter is the same old pressure, dressed up as a game. If something is free and asks you to connect your wallet, be suspicious first and research afterwards. If someone asks you to share your screen while you claim the prize, it's even more serious, because they can see everything you do.

**[CENA 4]** A simulated approval window on screen. The word "unlimited" is highlighted, with an arrow pointing to the "Approve" button.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Here's the technical detail most people never read. When you use some decentralized applications, the wallet asks for an approval. It's an authorization for a contract to move certain tokens you hold. Often, the approval comes with an unlimited amount already selected by default. If you approve a malicious contract with unlimited permission, it can withdraw those tokens whenever it likes, without asking you again. So always read what you're authorizing. And revoke the approvals you no longer use, with the official permission-revocation tools.

**[CENA 5]** Illustrative case. A student bedroom in Braga, Gonçalo, twenty-six, watching the countdown of an airdrop on his laptop. Caption: "illustrative case".
**NARRAÇÃO:** Let me tell you an illustrative case. Gonçalo, twenty-six, a student in Braga, received a link to claim an airdrop. The page was attractive, it had a countdown, and even a list of "winners" that kept updating. He connected his wallet, approved something he didn't understand and went to bed. Days later, he noticed tokens were missing. Nobody had asked him for his seed phrase, but he had given permission to whoever wanted it. The mistake wasn't technical. It was haste, and haste is exactly what these sites are designed to provoke.

**[CENA 6]** A screen with three questions in a row: "Who's asking? Why now? What am I authorizing?"
**NARRAÇÃO:** Before any connection or approval, ask yourself three questions. Who is asking? If you don't have a clear answer, that's a warning sign. Why is this happening now? If the justification is urgency, or a prize that expires today, be suspicious. And what am I authorizing, exactly? If you can't answer that precisely, don't sign. Every signature is a decision, not a courtesy click. You can hold on to a simple rule, and it's worth repeating: if you don't understand it, don't sign it. No prize is worth losing your wallet. And if you've already signed something suspicious, revoke that permission as soon as you can and check your balances. That doesn't mean everything is lost, but it does mean you need to act now, not tomorrow. In the last lesson of this module we'll look at the hardest part of all: the scam that is simply a friendly conversation.

**Texto no ecrã:** address letter by letter · an ad is not a guarantee · airdrop by message = alarm · "unlimited" = caution · if you don't understand it, don't sign
**Pergunta de verificação:** What do you do when a page asks you to approve an unlimited amount? Answer: you don't approve blindly. You confirm what you're authorizing and, if you don't understand it, you don't sign.

## Aula 5.4 — Romance, pressure and the 10-point checklist (≈4–5 min)
**Gancho (0:00–0:20):** The scam that's hardest to stop doesn't start with a website. It starts with a friendly conversation that gets closer and closer.

**[CENA 1]** A dating app screen with a smiling photo and the line: "I met you by chance, you seemed so nice".
**NARRAÇÃO:** There's a scam that begins slowly. Someone meets you on a dating app, or in a group chat, and the relationship grows closer over several weeks. You share routines, photos, jokes and confidences. When the subject of money comes up, the person talks about a crypto investment that's going really well for them, and offers to let you in too. They show you screenshots of profits and a platform with a beautiful dashboard. The closer the relationship gets, the harder it is to be suspicious. It comes disguised as friendship, and that's what makes it so dangerous. Look at the dashboard. Those numbers are designed to look real, and whoever shows them to you controls everything you see. It proves nothing. A platform that only exists inside a screen someone else shows you doesn't prove a profit, it only proves that somebody knows how to draw a chart. If you can't withdraw the money without paying more fees, or without waiting weeks for a new verification, the problem is there, not in your bad luck.

**[CENA 2]** An animated checklist, with the points appearing one by one, each with a tick.
**NARRAÇÃO:** So let's go through the ten-point checklist, which you can print and keep. One: nobody asks you for your seed phrase, your password or a code, ever. Two: you type the address by hand or use a verified bookmark, and you never click a link that reached you by message. Three: the platform is authorized under MiCA, the European regulation for crypto-asset service providers, and you can confirm that on the register of the authority in the country where it operates. Four: you turn on two-factor authentication with an app, not with SMS. Five: you use a unique password, stored in a password manager. Six: you're suspicious of any guaranteed return or any doubling. Seven: time pressure is an alarm, not an incentive.

**[CENA 3]** The checklist continues, with points eight to ten appearing on screen.
**NARRAÇÃO:** Eight: you never approve a contract you don't understand. Nine: you never install remote-access software at the request of a stranger, because whoever controls your screen controls everything you do. Ten: before you move any money, you wait twenty-four hours and talk to someone you trust. That last rule protects more people than any software does. Haste is the fuel of every scam, and waiting is the cheapest antidote there is.

**[CENA 4]** An animation of five concentric rings, each with a short label. Chart ID: camadas-seguranca.
**NARRAÇÃO:** No single defense is enough, so think in layers. The first layer is your email account, with a strong, unique password. The second is app-based authentication on the platforms you use. The third is a verified address, always the same one, saved in your bookmarks. The fourth is withdrawal limits and the allowed-address list, when the platform offers that option. And the fifth, the most important one for large amounts, is keeping most of it in a cold wallet, away from any screen connected to the internet. If one layer fails, the next one still protects you.

**[CENA 5]** A screen with a large clock and the line: "Is the person not letting you think? You already have the answer."
**NARRAÇÃO:** When someone pressures you to decide today, you're not talking to a friend who wants to help you. You're talking to someone who needs you not to think. A serious investment can withstand a week of reflection, and an honest project doesn't get angry when you ask for time. If the person gets irritated, disappears or makes you feel guilty for hesitating, you already have your answer. Your calm is the best defense you have, and it costs nothing.

**[CENA 6]** A screen with the title "Module mission" and a box to tick.
**NARRAÇÃO:** The module's mission is simple and concrete. This week, print the ten-point checklist and place it next to your computer. Check whether your platforms have app-based authentication. Review the active approvals in the wallets you use, and revoke any you don't recognize. And write your seed phrase on paper, by hand, in a safe place, without photographing it or sending it to anyone. When you've done all of this, tick the word "done". This is the first key to your shield, and it's the one that matters most.

**Texto no ecrã:** a friendly conversation is not proof · ten points · layers, not walls · haste is the alarm · mission: review your accounts
**Pergunta de verificação:** Which layer protects a large amount the most? Answer: keeping most of it in a cold wallet, away from a screen connected to the internet.

Roughly every four years, the creation of new bitcoins is cut in half. Do you know why, and do you know what that really means?

New bitcoins are created by miners, as a reward for validating blocks of transactions. That reward is not fixed. It is set in the protocol itself: every 210,000 blocks, which takes about four years on average, the reward per block is cut in half. This is called the halving. When the network started, in 2009, the reward was fifty bitcoins per block. In 2012 it dropped to twenty-five. In 2016 it went to twelve point five, in 2020 to six point two five, and in 2024 to three point one two five. Each step cuts the new supply in half. Notice that the rule does not depend on any one person: it is written in the code and applies in the same way to everyone.

With this rule, the total supply converges towards the limit of twenty-one million bitcoins. Most of it has already been issued, and what remains comes out in smaller and smaller slices, almost unnoticed. This is a fact about new supply, and that is what makes the topic interesting. Fewer new bitcoins per day means, in theory, less supply entering the market. Miners also pay for energy and equipment, so the drop in reward has a big effect on their accounts. But theory and price are different things, and this is where many people get it wrong. Supply is one piece of the puzzle. Demand, context and the mood of all the participants are the other pieces.

It is tempting to look at past halvings and conclude that the price always rises after each one. That conclusion is dangerous. There are too few cases to draw a rule from, the context changed a lot from one cycle to the next, and the market may have anticipated the event months earlier. Each halving is a fact about supply, not a date marked for a price rise. Use it as information to understand the market, never as a certainty about what will happen.

I want to tell you an illustrative case. Marta, 38, a teacher in Aveiro, watched a video about the halving and became convinced that a rise was certain. She bought everything at once, in one night, with money she had set aside for the car. In the following weeks the price fell, and she was left staring at the screen, not knowing what to do. She did not sell in a panic, but she did not have a plan either. The mistake was not the halving, which is a real fact. The mistake was treating a date as a guarantee, and using money that should not have been used. If the purchase had been made in instalments, with a written plan, she would have had time to review her decisions at every step.

A technical note, because it is easy to get confused. Not every network works this way. Bitcoin uses proof of work, with miners competing with energy and equipment. Ethereum moved to proof of stake in September 2022, in the event known as The Merge, and it no longer has mining or halvings. Each network has its own issuance rule. Before you say that something will rise because it has a halving, first ask yourself whether that network has a halving at all, and what kind.

So what does the halving teach you that is useful? It teaches you to think in long cycles and in supply, not in the news of the day. It also teaches you to be wary of anyone who sells the halving as a money machine. There is a lot of content that turns a date into a show, with countdown clocks and dramatic music. You do not need any of that. You need to understand the rule, keep the fact in mind, and decide what to do with your money based on a plan, not on a countdown. That brings us to the topic of the next lesson: how to read the market without guessing.

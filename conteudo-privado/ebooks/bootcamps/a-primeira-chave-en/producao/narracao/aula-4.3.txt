Leaving crypto on a platform is a bit like leaving money in a bank. It's convenient. But who holds the key?

There's a phrase that veterans repeat: not your keys, not your coins. In plain terms, it means: if you don't hold the keys, you don't hold the coins. Let's understand what that means, without dramatizing it, because the phrase doesn't mean that leaving crypto on a platform is automatically a mistake. It means you need to know what you're accepting when you do it.

When you buy crypto on a platform and leave it there, the platform keeps the keys for you. This is called third-party custody. It's the most convenient option. You recover your password if you forget it, you see your balance on the screen, and you can sell with one click. For many people, this convenience is exactly what they're looking for, and there's nothing wrong with that, as long as the choice is made knowingly.

The price of that convenience is that you start depending on the platform. If the platform has a technical problem, if it's attacked, if it's badly run, or if it stops operating, your access can be blocked. In some cases, the money may not come back. It isn't the most common scenario, but it's exactly what the word custody means: someone looks after what belongs to you, and you have to trust that they do. Think of a traditional bank: the money is in your name, but the bank has the systems, and you trust it to keep them secure. With crypto it's similar, only the regulation and the recovery rights vary a great deal from case to case.

And there's the opposite too. A personal wallet, with no custody, gives you full control. But if you lose the key, you lose everything, and there's nobody to ask for help. The risk moves from whoever holds it for you to yourself. Notice that the word recover can mean two things: recovering your account, when you've forgotten your password, and recovering your money, when something goes wrong. The first is normal. The second depends on who holds it and on the rules you accepted when you signed up. There's no abstract right choice. There's the right choice for your situation, for your amount, for the way you handle technology.

Many people use a hybrid approach. They leave on the platform the amount they use for trading, and move to a cold wallet the amount they want to keep for months. It isn't the only way to do it, but it's a sensible way to separate what moves from what rests. And separating is often the best protection there is. Remember the essentials: separating isn't running away. Someone who separates has a plan for each part, and that's what sets management apart from improvisation.

Here's a practical rule. The more value you have, the less waiting time you accept, and the greater the need for self-custody. The less value and the more you use it, the more sense it makes to be on a regulated platform, with support and account recovery. Nobody is forcing you to pick one extreme. You can sit somewhere in the middle, as long as you know where you stand. Before you leave value anywhere, read the custody terms, find out who is responsible and how an account is recovered. If you can't find that information, it's one more question to ask before you go ahead.

Illustrative case: Rui, a teacher from Leiria, had everything on one platform and had never thought about it. When he understood the difference, he didn't change everything overnight. It isn't about being better or worse. It's about knowing where your responsibilities lie. He started by moving the part he wasn't using into a cold wallet, and left the rest where it was for trading. He made the change calmly, and wrote down each step. It took a few weeks, and he says it was the best use of his time in crypto so far.

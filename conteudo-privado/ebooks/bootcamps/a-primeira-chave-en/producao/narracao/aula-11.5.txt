Leverage sounds like an accelerator. But if it were only an accelerator, what would stop the car? The answer is the stop and the size of the position, not the number on the screen.

We have reached the lesson that sums up level three, and perhaps the most important one in the module. When someone tells you they will use five times, or twenty times, the right question is not what the leverage is. The right question is: how much do I lose if the stop is hit? That question has an answer that does not depend on the leverage. It depends on two things: the distance to the stop and the size of the position. Leverage only tells you how much margin you need to hold that position. We will see why, with sums and with illustrative examples, of course.

The formula fits on a napkin: the loss, if the stop is hit, equals the notional multiplied by the stop distance as a percentage. That is all. Leverage does not enter into it. What enters is the size of the position, which is the notional, and the point where you are willing to get out. With this, the risk per trade is defined before you open. If you want to lose at most one per cent of your account, you choose the notional and the stop distance so that the loss, in that case, is one per cent. Leverage then becomes a consequence of that choice, not the starting point.

Let us take the same account of one thousand euros, with a risk of ten euros per trade, which is one per cent. First case: a stop at two per cent from the entry price. Notional: ten divided by two per cent gives five hundred euros. Leverage, against the account, half a time. Second case: a stop at one per cent. Notional of one thousand euros, leverage of one time. Third case: a stop at half a per cent. Notional of two thousand, leverage of two times. In all three, if the stop is hit, you lose exactly ten euros. What changed was the notional and the leverage, not the risk. This is the point I want you to keep.

An important caveat: the stop is an order, and an order can be executed at a worse price than the one you wrote, if the market makes a sudden jump. In a sharp move, the close can come out further away than the level you marked. So the stop reduces the risk, but does not eliminate it. And liquidation still exists, because if the margin is too small for the stop distance, the platform closes the position before the stop is reached. The rule is not “I set a stop and I can relax”. The rule is: the stop sits far enough away for normal noise, and the position is small enough that a jump does not wipe out your account.

Now the level three rule, which is not negotiable. This level is done only in a simulated account, MTM Funded or WebTrader, until you have completed thirty trades with a full journal. Thirty, not three. The journal records the date, the plan, the stop, the notional, the risk in euros, the fee, the reason for entry, the reason for exit and what you learned. Only after thirty trades can you think about a real account, and that decision is yours, never made with rent money. Mission for this lesson: open your simulated account, do the risk calculation with the model from this lesson, record the first lines of the journal, and write at the top of the sheet: zero of thirty.

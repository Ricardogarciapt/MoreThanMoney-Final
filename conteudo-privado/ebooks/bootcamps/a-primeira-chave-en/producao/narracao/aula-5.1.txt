Imagine you get a message from a platform's support team saying your account has been blocked and you have to act right now. If that makes you nervous, this lesson is for you.

Let's start with the most common scam, and also the easiest one to stop. Someone messages you on social media or in a group chat and presents themselves as a platform's support team. They say your account has been blocked, that there's an urgent verification, and that they need to confirm something. The message looks real. It has the right logo, the right language and even a reference number. But it's a lie, and the urgency is the first clue. Whoever is trying to catch you needs you to make a quick mistake, without thinking twice. That's why the countdown clock isn't a detail. It's their main weapon.

Here's the rule that is never broken. No legitimate support team will ask you for your seed phrase. They won't ask for your password, or for the code that arrives by SMS or in the app. And they won't write to you first to say you have a problem. The seed phrase, those twelve or twenty-four words you keep to recover your wallet, is the key to everything you hold there. Whoever has it has the money. There are no exceptions, not for support, not for a technician who calls you, not for anyone who says they work with the platform. If a message asks you to confirm something you never even looked for, the answer is no.

Let me tell you an illustrative case. Inês, twenty-nine, a nurse in Coimbra, received a message like this one on an afternoon off, while she was having a coffee. The support name looked official, and they asked her to validate her account through a website. She was suspicious for a simple reason: the message arrived without her having asked anyone for help. Instead of clicking, she closed the conversation, opened the app she already had installed and looked for herself. There was no block at all. There was a trap waiting for one tap.

That's exactly what Inês did, and it's what you should do too. If you think there's a problem with an account, don't use the link you received. Open the official app, or type the website address by hand into your browser. Look for the information there. If the problem is real, you'll find it in the right place, in the security area or in the notifications. If nothing shows up, the message was fake, and you stopped it without losing anything. This habit seems small, but it's what separates people who lose money from people who don't. Also save the official address in your bookmarks, so you can use it next time without relying on anyone.

Keep this sentence: if someone asks for your key, it's a scam, even if they look like support. Don't reply out of politeness, don't send a test message and don't click to sort it out quickly. Block the contact, report it on the platform where it appeared, and warn anyone who might be in the same situation, especially older family members, who are a favorite target for these messages. Scammers buy lists of accounts and phone numbers at random, and send thousands of identical messages. It isn't a personal attack, it's a volume business, which is why the best response is to give them nothing at all. If by any chance you've already given away your seed phrase, consider the wallet compromised. Create a new wallet on a clean device, and move what you can as quickly as possible. Then contact the platform. Every minute counts, because the person who scammed you is in a hurry too.

To sum up: nobody legitimate asks for your key, urgency is always a clue, and the link in a message is never the right way in. In the next lesson we'll look at the promise to double your money. It seems very different, but it's the same story in different clothes, and the maths behind it is even more revealing.

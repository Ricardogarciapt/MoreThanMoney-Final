A perpetual is a contract that never expires. It sounds simple, and the part that does not expire is exactly the part that will cost you attention.

Before any numbers, the warning that will be on screen throughout this module: educational content. The risk of loss is real. With perpetuals you can lose everything you put up as margin, and that happens faster than most people think. This module is for understanding, not for starting. Level three is optional. And before any real euro, what counts is the simulated account, as you have known since the start of the bootcamp. With that said, let us look at what a perpetual is. I want you to leave here with one clear sentence.

A normal futures contract has an expiry date. At the end of that date it is settled, and that is the end of it. A perpetual has no such date. It stays open for as long as you want it to, and as long as you have margin to keep it open. To stay close to the spot price, the perpetual uses a mechanism called funding. It is not a brokerage fee. It is a payment that happens from time to time, between those who are long and those who are short. When the perpetual trades above the spot price, those who are long pay those who are short. When it trades below, the opposite happens. It is the part most people ignore, and it weighs more than it seems on positions that stay open for days.

Let us look at an illustrative example. You have a long position with one thousand euros of notional value, which is the total size of the position, not the money you put in. The funding is zero point zero five per cent per payment period. Per period, you pay fifty cents. If the funding is positive, those who are long pay. If it is negative, those who are long receive. The number is only there to make the sum, and funding changes over time. What matters is the mechanism: someone who stays in a position for a long time with the funding working against them pays a small amount every day, and that does not show up on the candlestick chart.

Why does the perpetual stay close to the spot price? Because funding pushes. When there are many people long and the perpetual trades above the real price, funding turns positive, and those who are long pay to keep their position. That tends to cool demand on the long side. The reverse happens when many people are short: funding turns negative, and the shorts are the ones who pay. It is not magic, it is an incentive. In practice, funding is a cost or an income that runs while the position is open, and its sign changes with the market's conditions.

Two-minute exercise, on paper. A long position of one thousand euros of notional, with funding of zero point zero five per cent per period, and three periods a day, which is one of the common formats. How much do you pay in twenty-four hours? Three periods of fifty cents make one euro and fifty cents. One euro and fifty cents a day, on a notional of one thousand. An illustrative example, of course. Now repeat the sum with the position open for a whole week. If the funding works against you every day, how much do you pay in total?

Before money, people swapped things. Imagine you're a fisherman in Peniche. You take a basket of horse mackerel to the bakery, because you need bread. But what if the baker doesn't want fish that day? Or what if the fish spoils before he wants it? Economists call this the lack of a double coincidence of wants: the person who has what you want doesn't always want what you have. Direct swapping works, but it's slow and depends a lot on luck.

That's why, throughout history, people chose objects that almost everyone accepted. There were shells, salt, livestock, metals. Then came coins, and after them paper notes. Today, most money isn't even paper. It's a number on your bank's screen. You pay for a coffee with your phone, you send money through a payment app, and none of it passes from hand to hand. It's still money, because you still trust the system that recorded it.

Notice what this means. If trust is the foundation of everything, whoever controls the system controls that trust. A central bank can create more units of currency. A government can change the tax rules. I'm not saying this is bad by definition. I'm saying it's a human choice, made by human institutions, and those choices reach everyone's wallet. As long as the three pillars are standing, the note is worth something. If one of them starts to tremble, the conversation changes.

The first big consequence of money being a promise is called inflation. You'll see it in the next lesson, with a coffee and a simple sum anyone can do in their head. For now, keep this idea: promises can be kept or broken, slowly and quietly.

Before we finish, a two-minute exercise. Take a note, or look at your bank balance. Ask yourself: what do I believe in, to accept this as value? Write the answer by hand. There's no right answer. The goal is for you to realise that you already trust many things without thinking about it.

Your first crypto purchase might ask for your ID card, a selfie and your tax number. Strange? It isn't. It's there for a reason.

Before you buy anything, you need a platform. It's called an exchange, or trading platform. It's where you swap euros for cryptocurrencies and back again. And at this stage, the choice is the most important decision you'll make in the whole bootcamp. More important than the price, the coin or the timing. Why do I say that? Because most of the serious losses people suffer don't start with the market. They start with the platform.

A platform that isn't authorized can run off with your money, freeze your account or lie about where the funds are. It doesn't have to be a sophisticated fraud. It just has to have nobody supervising what it does. Think of it this way: a restaurant with a license from the city council and one without a license can serve the same food. Only one of them has someone answerable to the law if something goes wrong. So the first test is simple: is the platform authorized, and does an official body supervise it? If the answer is no, the conversation ends here.

In the European Union there is the MiCA regulation, which we've already talked about. Platforms that provide crypto-asset services need authorization and are supervised. In Portugal, the body that supervises the securities markets is the CMVM, the Portuguese securities market regulator. Go to the official website of that body, or of the authority in the country where the platform says it's based, and check whether the platform appears on the register of authorized providers. If it isn't there, it doesn't matter how nice the website looks.

Don't rely only on a logo, an advert or an influencer who gets paid to talk about a platform. Trust the official register. Write down the platform's exact name, search for it in the register, and compare the name and the website domain with what's listed there. It sounds like extra work, and it's exactly the work that scammers hope you won't do. One minute of checking is worth more than any promotion that reaches you by message.

Now, KYC. The letters stand for know your customer, and it's the identity verification process. You'll need to provide an identity document, often a selfie holding that document, and your tax number. It might feel intrusive. But it's exactly what the law requires to fight money laundering and fraud. It's also the difference between a serious platform and one that just wants your money with no questions asked. Anyone who asks you for documents is doing their duty. Anyone who asks for nothing deserves a second look. Notice that this isn't distrust of you. Banks do exactly the same when you open an account, and nobody finds that strange. In crypto, a lot of people still think anonymity is a feature. Almost always, it's a risk signal.

It's worth spotting a warning sign. If a platform lets you deposit without verifying your identity, be wary. If it asks you for money off the books, or to unlock an account, or to pay a fee you've never seen before, set it aside. KYC isn't an obstacle. It's a sign that someone is doing their homework. And a message rushing you to deposit is almost always a message from someone who wants your money before you've had time to think.

Illustrative case: Gonçalo, thirty-two, a maintenance technician in Setúbal, was about to deposit money into a platform that had reached him through a message. It looked well made, and it even had a support contact with a name. Before going ahead, he checked the official register and couldn't find the platform. He didn't buy anything. He learned that the message was a trap. It sounds like a small thing, but it was the most valuable decision he made that month.

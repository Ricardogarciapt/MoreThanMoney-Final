Imagine a notebook that the whole village has, and that nobody can tear up without everyone noticing.

Imagine a village where everyone has an account book. Every time someone owes money to another person, a line is written: who paid, who to, how much, and the date. Only here there's one rule. There isn't a single notebook kept by the head of the parish council. Everyone has a copy, and the copies are updated together. That is, in very simple terms, a blockchain: a chain of blocks, where each block is a page with several lines.

Let's go step by step. First, the lines. A transaction says: this wallet sent so many units of this currency to that wallet. Second, the pages. A block bundles several transactions and closes when it's full, or when a certain amount of time has passed. Third, the link between pages. Each page has a kind of seal that results from the content of the page before it. Technically it's called a hash function, but think of it as a fingerprint of the content: if the content changes, the fingerprint changes. If someone alters an old line, the seal no longer matches, and all the following pages become invalid.

This is what makes the notebook hard to erase. It's not that changing anything is impossible. It's that changing it means redoing the whole path, and it has to be accepted by the majority of the network. And that majority has copies of the notebook. Anyone trying to tamper with an old page has to convince the whole village that the story is different. In practice, that doesn't happen.

But then, who writes the new page? This is where many people find things confusing, and I'll simplify it. There are two main methods. The first is called proof of work, or mining. Computers compete to solve a difficult mathematical problem. Whoever solves it first writes the next page and receives a reward. It uses a lot of energy, and it's the method Bitcoin uses. The second is called proof of stake. Instead of competing with calculation, participants lock up coins as collateral, and the system chooses who writes the page. If that person tries to cheat, they lose the collateral. This is the method the Ethereum network adopted in September 2022, in a transition called The Merge.

Notice what the two have in common. Nobody writes in the notebook without paying a cost, whether it's energy or collateral. And nobody decides on their own what is true. That's the big idea behind a blockchain: a shared record without a central authority having the last word.

Now an important warning. A blockchain solves a specific problem: keeping a shared record without a central entity. That doesn't mean the project is safe, or that the currency is good, or that the price will rise. Solid technology can be attached to a badly designed product. We'll separate these things throughout the course, and from module 2 onwards you'll have tools to do that.

Illustrative example, to close. Inês wanted to understand this with her husband, who works in logistics, and he asked her why nobody could erase the notebook. She answered with a simple three-step exercise: draw four pages, with two lines each, write on each seal a word that summed up the previous page, and then change one line on the first page to see what happened to the others. He got it in two minutes. You'll need a little more, and that's fine. You don't need to understand the maths to understand the logic. You just need to see why changing the past is so costly.

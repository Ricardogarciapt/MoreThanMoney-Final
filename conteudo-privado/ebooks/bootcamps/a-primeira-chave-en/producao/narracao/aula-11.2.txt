Two accounts with the same balance can react in completely different ways to the same fall. The difference lies in a choice that almost nobody reads.

Margin is the money you put up to hold the position. It is not the cost of the whole position, it is the collateral. When you open a leveraged perpetual, the platform requires an initial margin, and it is that margin which rises and falls with the market. There are two ways to organise it: isolated and cross. The difference is not in the leverage. It is in who pays when the position goes wrong. This choice sounds technical, but it is one of the most important risk decisions in this module, and it is often the one people make without thinking.

Isolated margin means each position has its own collateral. If you put one hundred euros into a position, only those one hundred euros are at risk. If the position is liquidated, you lose those hundred, and the rest of the account stays intact. Cross margin means all positions share the available balance. A position in trouble can use the money from the others to hold on. It sounds like an advantage, and sometimes it is, but it has a cost: a single bad position can drag the entire account down. For someone learning, that is a big problem.

Let us put the two side by side, with an illustrative example. You have a simulated account of one thousand euros and open two positions. With isolated margin, you give one hundred euros to each. If the first goes wrong and is liquidated, you lose those hundred, and the second carries on with its hundred. With cross margin, both use the full balance of one thousand. If the first goes wrong, the second can use what is left, and liquidation only happens when the whole account reaches the limit. In other words, cross holds out for longer, but when it fails, it fails all together. The numbers are for the account, not for any real position.

This is my view, which you can argue with: for someone starting out, isolated margin is the choice I recommend. Someone starting has not yet tested a risk plan, and isolated forces you to decide how much each position gets. That limit is a lesson in itself. With cross, the account works like one single pot, and it is easy to give more margin to a position that is losing, in the hope that it recovers. That is called putting more money into something that is already going wrong. Cross has its place for those who already have written and tested rules, but it is not where you start.

Three-minute exercise. If the platform you use allows it, do it in a simulated account: open two small positions with the same leverage, one in isolated margin and one in cross. If it does not allow it, do it on paper. Fill in the sheet: how much money is inside each position, what happens to the other one if the first goes wrong, and which of the two leaves you more at ease. There is no right answer to the last question, only yours. Next lesson we see what liquidation is and how it is calculated, in simplified form, with an illustrative sum.

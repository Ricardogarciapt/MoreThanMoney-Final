Bitcoin is a notebook of accounts. Ethereum tries to be a whole village, with rules that enforce themselves.

Bitcoin was designed, above all, to record who has what. Ethereum launched in 2015 with a different ambition: to let anyone write rules into the notebook, and to have those rules run by themselves, without an intermediary. It isn't just a record of balances. It's a kind of shared computer, where many people run programmes at the same time, and everyone confirms the result.

These rules are called smart contracts. The name is a bit misleading. They aren't contracts in the legal sense, and they aren't smart in the sense of thinking. They are programmes that run on the network. A helpful analogy is the vending machine. You insert a coin, choose an option, and the machine hands over the product. You don't need an employee to confirm it. The rule is inside, and it applies the same way to everyone. A smart contract works like this: if this happens, then that happens. When the condition is met, the action executes. Each of these programmes has the same skeleton: a rule, a condition and an automatic execution. The difference lies in what gets executed, and in who is responsible if something goes wrong.

This opens many doors. You can have automatic loans, exchange markets between people, games, digital collections. I won't tell you which of these will work. Not all of them do, and some should never have existed. Just because something is technically possible doesn't mean it's a good idea to use it, or that it has any real use for you.

And here comes the warning that is worth its weight in gold: code can fail too. A contract written in a hurry can contain a mistake, and mistakes on a blockchain are hard to correct, because the notebook can't be torn out. In 2016, a project called The DAO lost an enormous amount of ether because of a flaw in its code, and that led to a split of the network itself. I don't want to scare you with numbers. I want you to understand one thing: code is not a guarantee of anything on its own.

Now let's talk about the big change in 2022. Until September of that year, Ethereum used proof of work, like Bitcoin. Then it moved to proof of stake. That transition is called The Merge. The network stopped depending on computers competing through calculation, and started depending on participants who lock up coins as collateral. The change altered how the network uses energy and how new coins are issued. But it didn't remove the need to understand what you're buying. A better technology doesn't fix a badly designed project.

It's also worth understanding how Ethereum charges for operations. Every action on the network has a cost, called a fee, and that fee varies with demand. At busy moments, it can become expensive. If you ever send a small amount and are surprised by the cost, now you know why. Illustrative example: Inês wanted to send twenty euros from one wallet to another, and the fee ended up almost as high as the transfer itself. It wasn't a tragedy. It was a lesson. Also think about this: when the network is very full, each operation competes with the others for space, and whoever pays more goes first. So the same transfer can cost little on a calm morning and a lot in a busy afternoon. Before any operation, always check how much it's going to cost you.

Think of these as two different tools. A simple notebook is good for recording who owes what. A programme running on the network is good for executing complicated rules without an intermediary. Both are useful, and both have their own risks. The common mistake is to assume that complexity means quality. Often it's just more surface area for things to go wrong.

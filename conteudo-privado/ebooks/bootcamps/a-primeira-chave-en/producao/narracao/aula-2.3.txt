There's a crypto that is supposed to always be worth one dollar. It looks like a safe vault. Ask yourself: who holds the key to the vault?

A stablecoin is a digital coin that tries to hold a fixed value, normally one dollar. The best-known examples are USDC and USDT. It sounds like the ideal scenario: a digital dollar that moves across the network in seconds. But notice the word tries. A stablecoin has no magic guarantee. On the contrary, its value depends on a concrete promise, and that promise has to be kept by someone.

What a stablecoin promises is something specific: that, for each unit issued, there is an equivalent reserve, in cash or in low-risk assets, and that the issuer redeems it at the exact value. Think of a casino chip, or a supermarket voucher. The chip is worth what is printed on it because the casino honours it. If the casino stops honouring it, the chip loses value. A stablecoin works in the same way: its value depends on who issues it. An everyday example: a company meal card is only worth what the restaurant accepts, and the restaurant only accepts it while the system keeps paying it. Trust runs down in layers, and one weak layer drags the others down with it.

That's why there's a term you should know: issuer risk. Whoever issues the coin has to hold verifiable reserves, has to follow rules, and has to be able to redeem coins when people ask. If there are doubts about the reserves, or if many people try to redeem at the same time, the value can drift away from the dollar. That isn't a theoretical possibility. It's exactly the scenario that gets tested when the market panics.

This has already happened. In 2022, an algorithmic stablecoin, from an ecosystem that at one point became enormous, lost its peg to the dollar and dragged a lot of value down with it. It was not a coin backed by dollar reserves. It was a balancing mechanism between two digital coins. When that balance broke, the value collapsed. This story is the best argument for not trusting the word stable.

In the European Union, there is now a legal framework called MiCA, which has applied to crypto-asset service providers since thirty December 2024. It brings more rules, more transparency and more supervision. It's a real step forward. But rules are not price guarantees, and it's important not to confuse the two. A stablecoin can comply with regulation and still carry risks that depend on its reserves, its liquidity and the trust of the people who use it. Notice a practical difference: a regulation obliges companies to meet their duties. It doesn't oblige the coin to hold a fixed price. They are different things, and one doesn't replace the other.

Let's look at an illustrative example, with round, invented numbers. Imagine you hold one hundred units of a stablecoin that is worth one dollar. If the coin loses its peg and drops to ninety cents, your balance is now worth ninety dollars. Nothing dramatic at first glance. But if you were using it as a safe haven, you've lost ten percent at the exact moment you were looking for security. That's what makes the example uncomfortable.

Why do people use stablecoins? To get out of volatile positions quickly, to send value between countries, to hold a digital dollar for a period of time. These are legitimate uses. But none of them removes the risk. Illustrative example: Rui, forty-one years old, a teacher in Leiria, had put part of his savings into a stablecoin, convinced it was idle money with no risk. When he asked where the reserves were and who audited them, he realised he had assumed something without checking it. He didn't lose everything, but he learned to ask questions. If you have doubts about how a value behaves, the first question is always the same: who is obliged to redeem it, and under what conditions?

There are thousands of coins, and most of them will disappear. How do you tell a serious project from a joke with a logo?

We call altcoins all the cryptocurrencies that are not Bitcoin. There are many, with very different goals. Some try to be better payment systems, others want to connect data chains, others want to be platforms for applications. And then there are memecoins, which deserve a section of their own. A reminder: this is educational content, and not financial advice. The risk of loss is real, and in crypto it is often total.

A memecoin is often born from a joke, an image or a social media trend. As a rule, it doesn't solve a concrete problem. Its value depends almost entirely on the attention it receives. When the attention changes, the price changes. This can happen in hours, sometimes in minutes. Think of a soap bubble at a birthday party: it's pretty, everyone points at it, and it bursts without warning. And notice a word that keeps coming up: attention. If the value comes from attention, whoever sells early wins, and whoever buys late loses.

I'm not saying all of them are fraud. I'm saying the risk is extremely high and that, often, the marketing is the only product. When someone sells you a coin with a guaranteed profit, the right response is to be suspicious. No honest project can guarantee a return, and anyone who says it can is trying to stop you asking questions.

So that you don't rely on instinct, I'm going to give you a tool: seven questions. They aren't a formula, they're a filter. If you can't answer one of them, you're not ready to buy. First: what problem does this project solve? If the answer is just it's going to go up, that's a bad sign. Second: who is behind it, and is that public? An anonymous team isn't necessarily fraud, but it raises the risk, and you need to know that before deciding. A project with identified people who publicly stand behind what they do carries a different kind of risk. Neither of them is free of risk.

Third: what is the total supply, and who holds it? If a small number of wallets hold most of the coins, the price can be manipulated easily. Fourth: is there real utility, or only speculation? Ask who uses the project for anything other than buying and selling. Fifth: who controls the code, and can they change it? If one person can change the rules without warning, you need to know that before you get in. A supplementary question worth asking: who decides on updates, and how are those decisions communicated?

Sixth: where is it traded, and is there enough liquidity to sell? An asset without buyers is an asset you can't sell at the price you see on the screen. Seventh, and perhaps the most useful: what happens if the project disappears tomorrow? If the answer is nothing, I lose everything, you already know how much you can risk. If you have no way of selling, the chart on the screen is just an opinion. These seven questions won't give you certainties. They'll give you better questions than the ones the advertising asks you.

Illustrative example, with invented numbers. Imagine a coin that promises high yield, has a founder with no face, holds ninety percent of its supply in a single wallet, and has no use outside the coin itself. You've answered badly to almost all of them. You don't need anything more to decide not to enter. And if, on the other hand, the answers were good, they still wouldn't oblige you to buy. A good answer only tells you it's worth studying more, never that the price will go up. Also watch out for requests: if someone asks you to send crypto to unlock a sum of money, or pressures you to decide today, that isn't analysis. It's pressure. We'll talk about scams in more detail in module 5. Notice that I haven't asked you to get it right. I've asked you not to be carried away by haste, and to understand what you don't know yet.

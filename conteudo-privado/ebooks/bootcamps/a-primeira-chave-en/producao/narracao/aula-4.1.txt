A wallet doesn't store coins. It never has. It stores keys. And whoever holds the key holds the money.

Let's correct something right away, because it's the most common confusion. A crypto wallet isn't a safe with coins inside. The coins live on the blockchain, the shared ledger we talked about in module 1. The wallet holds the keys that let you sign transactions, and those keys are what give you access to the value. Once you understand this, the way you think about security changes, because you stop looking for where the coins are and start looking for where the keys are. Think of an everyday analogy: your bank card number doesn't give you money, but anyone who has it and knows the code can spend. With crypto, the key plays that role, only there's no counter and no complaints department.

That's why the key is the metaphor for this bootcamp. In crypto, whoever holds the key holds the money. There's no manager you can ask for a replacement, and no counter where you can explain that you lost your card. If the key is lost, access is lost. If the key is stolen, the value goes with it. That may sound harsh, but it's exactly what gives meaning to the phrase being the owner of your money. Being the owner also means being responsible.

There are two main types of wallet. The first is the hot wallet. It's an app or a program connected to the internet. It's convenient, quick, and good for everyday operations. The risk is that, because it's connected, it's more exposed: to an infected phone, a fake website, a copied app that looks identical to the original. Convenience and exposure always go together. A well-built app isn't insecure for that reason, but you need to check every time where you downloaded it from.

The second is the cold wallet. It's a physical device, often the size of a USB stick, that keeps your keys offline. To sign a transaction, you plug it in and confirm on the device itself. A well-known commercial example is Ledger, which is sold in the MTM shop. The advantage is that the key never leaves the device. The downside is that it's less convenient, and if you lose it without a backup, the problem remains. We'll get to that.

Here's a simple comparison. A hot wallet is like having cash in your pocket: it's within reach, and it's good for buying a coffee. A cold wallet is like a safe at home, with a key that only you use, for your savings. Neither one is the universal answer. It depends on how much you have, how often you move it, and how much risk you're willing to take. The chart shows the trade-off: the more convenience, the more exposure. It isn't a quality ranking. A cold wallet isn't better in every situation; it's better for certain situations.

There's a practical rule worth keeping. Small amounts you use often can live in a hot wallet. Amounts you won't touch for months deserve a cold one. And no wallet protects you from your own mistakes, like sending to the wrong address, or signing something you didn't understand. Technical security doesn't replace attention. Before you confirm any transaction, read what you're confirming. Also note that a cold wallet won't save you from scams. If someone convinces you to sign a malicious transaction on the device itself, the technology is there, but you're the one deciding.

Be careful: the wallet address is public, like an account number. You can share it to receive funds. But the private key and the recovery phrase never. Illustrative case: Tiago had almost everything in a phone app. One day he lost the device. He managed to recover the account because he had the recovery phrase stored, but he spent a whole weekend sorting it out. He learned that convenience has a price, and that the price is paid in time, stress and attention.

If you keep a thousand euros under the mattress for ten years, what can they buy at the end?

Let's do a sum. A coffee costs one euro today. It sounds like very little, and it is very little. Now imagine prices rise four percent a year. This is a round, hypothetical number, just so we can do the maths. It's an illustrative example, nothing more. After one year, the coffee costs one euro and four cents. After five years, it costs about one euro and twenty-two. After ten years, it reaches one euro and forty-eight. Nobody notices anything special. It's a cent here, another there, and suddenly the same coffee costs almost half as much again.

Now think about your money. If you have a thousand euros sitting in a drawer, ten years from now you still have a thousand euros on paper. But those thousand euros no longer buy what they used to. To keep being able to buy in ten years what a thousand euros buys today, you'd need about one thousand four hundred and eighty euros. Put the other way round: today's thousand euros are worth, in purchasing power, about six hundred and seventy-six euros.

Notice that this sum doesn't need a special year or a crisis. A small, steady inflation is enough. A rate of two percent, which is the European Central Bank's medium-term reference, already cuts money's purchasing power in half in about thirty-six years. Nobody feels that in a month. You feel it over a whole lifetime.

There's a practical rule worth memorising. It's called the rule of 72. You divide 72 by the inflation rate, and you get, in years, how long it takes for money to lose half its purchasing power. At four percent, that's eighteen years. It's an approximation, but it gives you the order of magnitude. And the order of magnitude is what you need to decide well. When you hear someone say inflation isn't anything anymore, come back to this sum. A small number, repeated for years, is a huge force.

Let's be honest. Inflation isn't anyone's plan to harm you. It's a phenomenon that results from many decisions: demand, costs, monetary policy. But its effect always arrives the same way. Inês had three thousand euros in a current account earning practically nothing. She didn't want to risk everything, and she didn't want to watch her money shrink either. The first thing she did was understand the problem, not choose a product. That's the step I want you to take too. Before any decision, know how much money you have, how long you can leave it idle, and what the effect of inflation is in that specific case.

This matters for the rest of the bootcamp, because it's the first reason people look for alternatives. It's not because they want to get rich quickly. It's because they want their savings not to melt away in silence. So throughout this bootcamp, whenever we talk about returns, you'll ask one simple question: is it a gain above inflation, or just a nice-looking number on the screen? But be careful: looking for an alternative doesn't mean any alternative will do. Some assets rise a lot and fall even faster. In the next lessons, we'll see how to tell them apart.

Fourteen modules later, the honest question is this: what do you really take with you?

Let's review the whole course in a few minutes, by level. Level one, fundamentals, modules one to five: money and blockchain, the map of coins, the first safe purchase, your key and the anti-scam shield. Level two, investor with the MTM Smart DCA, modules six to ten: the cycles and the halving, your DCA with the house tools, the Portfolio with the reserve pot, the reading of the market and taking profits with written rules. Level three, optional, modules eleven to fourteen: perpetuals, the Aurum Flow Cripto strategy, risk and position size, and executing without emotion. Three levels, one idea: each one depends on the one before.

Level one review. The mission was a first purchase, small, on an authorised platform, a test transfer to your wallet, and the anti-scam checklist signed. If the three boxes are ticked, level one is closed. If they are not, don't move on. A purchase made without the key safely stored and without the checklist is a risk you don't need to take. Notice that I did not ask you for a result. I asked you for a process: verify, store, confirm and record. That is what you can control, and it is what the course has taught you to do.

Level two review: a written DCA plan, with monthly cap and reserve pot, DCA alerts switched on, and four weeks in a row of the Friday ritual, recorded. And level three, if you did it: thirty simulated trades with a complete journal and written stopping rules. Ask yourself, for each mission: what is written down? If the answer is only I think so, the mission is not closed. The course asks you for paper, not memory. That paper is the proof that you are learning, and it is also the only honest way to assess your own progress.

Now the concepts that cannot be missing, and that you should be able to explain without looking at your notes. Why the key is yours. What DCA is, and why it does not stop. What the reserve pot is. What settlement is, and why leverage does not create money. What the journal is for. And why a stopping rule exists before emotion shows up. If you cannot explain one of these concepts in your own words, go back to the corresponding module. You don't have to memorize. You have to understand. If you want, write each one in a sentence and compare it with the version in the corresponding module when you're done.

A typical temptation at the end of a course is to apply everything in the same week. Don't do that. Choose one thing you have learned and apply it for a month. It can be the security checklist, it can be the Friday ritual, it can be the journal. A small, constant practice is worth more than ten big intentions. And if you realise you are missing a module, go back to it before you move on. There is no prize for finishing first, and what matters is that you understand what you are doing. If you feel that urge, write it in the notebook and wait a week before acting.

Before the exam, five questions, written in the notebook. What has stayed with me? What am I still not following? Which rule will I change this week? Which mission still needs closing? And what do I want to ask the accountant, if I have tax doubts? Write the answers down, with the date. These five questions are your personal summary of the course, and they are worth more than any summary I could give you. The final exam is next. You don't have to answer them out loud, but you do have to write them down, with today's date, and keep them alongside the ninety-day plan. They are your starting point for what comes after the course, and it is worth doing them with time and without rushing.

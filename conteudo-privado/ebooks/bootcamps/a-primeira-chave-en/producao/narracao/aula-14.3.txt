If it isn't written down, it didn't happen. That sounds exaggerated, until we notice how many decisions we make from memory, and how often we recount them wrongly.

This lesson is about the trading journal. It is not a pretty dossier. It is a page where you write each trade before, during and after. The full template comes in the student notebook, but the idea is simple: a trade that is not in the journal, for learning purposes, did not exist. Because memory is generous with wins and cruel with mistakes. A week later, you remember having been right. You don't remember having entered outside the rule, or having moved the stop in a hurry. The journal exists to end that automatic selection our brain makes without asking permission.

Let's go through the fields. Before entering, you record the date, the asset and the reason for the entry, written in one sentence, and which rule you are applying. You record the size of the position and how much you lose if the stop is hit, always in euros, never only in percentage. And you record the stop and the targets. Before you touch the button, the journal already has everything. After closing, you add the result, whether the rule was followed, and one line about what you felt. Illustrative example: I entered because the price returned to the average; I lose twenty euros if the stop is hit; I followed the rule; I felt rushed at ten in the morning.

The most important column is the one almost nobody fills in: was the rule followed, yes or no. Because the goal at this stage is not to win. It is to execute the process you wrote. A losing trade with the rule followed is a good training trade. A winning trade outside the rule is a warning, not a compliment. If you mark no and the trade made a profit, write exactly that, without excuses. That no will be the most useful piece of information you get in a whole week, because it shows you where emotion got involved, even when it went well. Always write it, even when the answer bothers you.

Illustrative case: Rui, twenty-nine, made three trades in one week. On the first, he followed the rule and lost the expected amount, without drama. On the second, he moved the stop halfway through, because he didn't want to watch the loss grow. On the third, he entered with no written reason, just because the chart seemed to be moving fast. When he did the review, the last two lines had no in the rule column. That was when he understood the problem. It was not the market. It was him stepping out of the process, twice, in silence. The journal showed that in two minutes. Memory would have hidden it all.

Two rules so you don't spoil the journal. First: write before you enter. A journal written afterwards is a story, and stories adjust themselves. Second: never rewrite an old line. If you get a word wrong, you cross it out and write over it, with the date of the correction. If you erase what you did badly, you are erasing the only thing that can teach you. The journal is not there to judge you. It is there to show yourself what you really did, dated today and without makeup. Take this out of the notebook and keep it in view, so you can reread it before each session.

Imagine a house. You have a structure you never touch, and some rooms you decorate over the years. A crypto portfolio works in much the same way.

Before you set up anything, I want you to understand one thing. In a portfolio, the most important question is not which coin I buy. It is how much of my money goes into each piece, and why. This module is about that question. We are going to build the portfolio in layers, from the most stable to the riskiest, and each layer has its own role. If you have got this far, you already know what DCA is and you already have your plan, with a base amount, a fixed day and a ceiling. What is missing is giving shape to what you buy. And notice: we are talking about organisation and education. No portfolio gives you guarantees. The risk of loss is real, and it can be total.

The model we are going to use is called core and satellites. The core is the big part, the one you intend to hold for years. It is usually made up of the best-known assets with the longest history, and that is where your DCA spends most of its time. The satellites are the smaller part: smaller, more volatile assets, each with its own thesis, which you must be able to explain in two sentences. The core weathers the storms. The satellites are where you can regret things fastest. So the core is the foundation, and the satellites are an extra, with a cap.

Let us put numbers on it, so you can see the order of magnitude. Illustrative example: a plan of one hundred euros a month. Ninety euros go to the core and ten to the satellites. Then, within the satellites, no single asset gets more than a small slice, say a third of the satellite pie. These numbers are not a recommendation. They are a way of making visible a decision that, without paper, stays hidden in a Thursday-night impulse. Once the decision is written down, it is much harder to take it badly on impulse. Notice that the sum is always the same: first the total, then the split, and only at the end the name of each asset.

Every satellite needs a written thesis. Ask yourself: why is it here? What problem does it solve, or what bet am I making? If the answer is “because it went up a lot last week”, that is not a thesis, it is a headline. Write the thesis before you buy, and review it once a month. If you can no longer explain why it is there, take it out of the satellites. Careful: DCA does not stop because of that. It is the plan that changes, in the notebook, calmly, and never in the middle of a drop. The new money then goes somewhere else in the plan.

A very common trap is holding twelve assets with one-euro slices. It looks like diversification, but in practice it is a collection of decisions you do not understand. Anyone starting should have a core of one or two assets and, at most, two or three satellites. If you are thinking about more, wait. You can always add later, once you have the habit of writing the thesis and recording every purchase. Fewer assets, more thesis, and a DCA you actually follow, rather than a DCA lost on a screen full of coins.

Three-minute exercise. Take your plan and write three lines in the notebook. On the first, the core, with the asset and its percentage of the plan. On the second and third, the satellites, if you want to have them, with the thesis in two sentences each. If you have no satellites, write “no satellites” and move on. It is a valid decision, and often the most sensible one. Next lesson we will see how much to put into each piece and how stablecoins fit into this sum. There is no exact percentage that suits everyone. Anyone who tells you there is, is selling you something.

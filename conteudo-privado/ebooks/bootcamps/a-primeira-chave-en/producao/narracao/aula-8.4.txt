Copying is the shortest route to learning nothing. Looking carefully is slower, and gives you much more.

You already know the two house portfolios: one crypto and one ETF, topped up every Friday and recorded purchase by purchase. Today we are going to learn how to read them. Not to copy them, but to see a recorded structure working over time. A demonstration portfolio does not tell you what to do. It tells you what was done, with rules that, ideally, are written down, and it lets you ask why. That question is what is worth taking from this lesson. You do not need to memorise them. You need to open them with a clear goal, which is to learn the method and not to copy the result.

Look at three things. First, the structure: how many assets there are and which one carries the most weight. Second, discipline: the dates of the moves, all on a Friday. Third, consistency: compare each top-up with what the MTM Smart DCA indicated that day. If the portfolio follows its own rule, you can see it in the dates and in the labels. If it does not, ask why. A careful reader is not looking for the right answer. They look for the rule behind each move, and notice when it does not appear. Do this reading with the notebook open, not from memory, because memory tends to keep only what we like to see.

There is one distinction I want you to always make. What to copy: no assets, no percentages, no moments. What to learn: the order of things. First you write the structure. Then you define the rhythm. Only then do you top up. Another person's assets, or the house's, are the context of that portfolio, and your context is different: a different base amount, a different time horizon, a different tolerance for falls. Copying someone else's context is the fastest way to buy at the wrong bottom without understanding why. Once the distinction is clear, the house portfolio stops being a mirror of ambition and becomes a mirror of method.

When you look at a portfolio, ask it three questions. Why is this asset here? How much does it weigh, and has that changed over time? Which rule explains a change? If you find no answer, that is not a fault of yours. Often the answer is simply not written down. In that case, the portfolio is not an example for you, it is just a record. And that is fine: a record also teaches, as long as you know you are not looking at a manual. Write the answers with the date beside them, because in a few months you will want to compare your reading today with the one you make when you have more experience.

Use the house portfolio as a mirror. When you have your own table, put the two side by side. Ask: does my split follow the same logic? Does it have a core, capped satellites, a pot and a rule? If yours has something the house does not have, for example a different satellite, write down why. The difference is not an error. It is your decision, and that is what we want to see in the notebook. And remember: past results do not guarantee future results, neither those of the house nor yours. If any difference bothers you, do not erase it straight away. Write it down and leave it there for a week before you decide what to do.

Mission for this module: write your target portfolio in the notebook. One page. It has the core, with the asset and its weight. It has the satellites, if you have them, each with its thesis. It has the split in euros for your monthly plan, your cap per coin, the rebalancing method and the pot, with its exit rule. And it has a final line: what you would do if the core fell twenty per cent in one month. You do not need to buy anything to complete the mission. The mission is to write.

You buy a hundred euros of a crypto and, a minute later, it's worth less than a hundred euros. The price hasn't even moved. So what happened?

When you buy crypto, you pay more than the price you see on the screen. That's not a trick. These are costs, and it's worth knowing them before you buy, not after. Imagine you're buying an item of clothing in a shop, and at the till an extra amount appears that wasn't on the price tag. You don't feel cheated if you already knew that amount existed. You feel cheated when nobody warned you. There are three costs that almost everyone runs into. The first is the platform fee. It's a percentage of the value of the transaction, and it can vary depending on the payment method and the type of account. Always read the full fee table, not just the summary on the front page.

The second cost is the spread. It's the difference between the price at which you buy and the price at which you sell, at the same moment. If the buy price is a bit higher than the sell price, that difference is the spread, and it stays with the platform or with whoever is making the market. It sounds like a detail, but you feel it on every trade, even when you don't notice it.

The third cost, if you transfer to your own wallet, is the network fee. Every transfer on the blockchain has a cost, and it changes with how busy the network is. We've already seen this with Ethereum. It isn't the platform that charges it, it's the network itself. And anyone who doesn't know this is surprised when the amount that arrives is smaller than the amount they sent.

Let's do the sums with round, hypothetical numbers. Illustrative example. You buy one hundred euros. The platform charges a one percent fee. The spread, at that moment, is half a percent. You're paying around one and a half euros in costs. About ninety-eight euros and fifty cents is left, in market terms. That seems small. But look at it from the other side. A cost of one and a half euros on a hundred-euro purchase looks like rounding. Only, the cost doesn't sit still: it works against you from the first second.

If the price falls two percent straight after, your balance drops to around ninety-six euros and fifty cents. You need the price to rise by more than two percent just to get back to where you started. The costs push you backwards before the market gives you any gain. And the more often you buy and sell, the more the costs eat into the result. Every trade carries a small, invisible tax, and it adds up. This isn't a rule made up by sellers. It's arithmetic. Someone who gets in and out often has to earn more just to stay where they were. This example isn't special in any way: it's the same for anyone buying the same amount, with the same fees.

This is the part a lot of people skip. Think about the total cost of getting in and getting out. If you buy and sell, you pay twice. If you just hold, you pay once, but waiting also costs something, in risk and in opportunity. I'm not saying buying is bad. I'm saying the cost has to be on the sheet before the first click. If you only think about the price, you're seeing half the bill. And there's one detail that's worth its weight in gold: the cost of buying with a card is often different from the cost of transferring from a bank. Always ask which method is cheapest for your case, and compare against the official fee table, not against your memory.

Illustrative case: Inês made a small purchase and noticed the app showed a slightly lower value than what she had paid. She thought the app was wrong. It wasn't. It was the fees and the spread. After working it out with pen and paper, she understood everything, and from then on she looked at the cost before every trade. It was the best way for her to learn, because the mistake cost her very little.

There is a way of investing that almost nobody wants to hear about, because it is boring. That is exactly why it takes the guesswork out of the equation.

DCA stands for regular buying, or dollar cost averaging. The idea is simple. Instead of investing everything at once, you buy a fixed amount at regular intervals. It can be one hundred euros a month, or twenty euros a week. You buy when the price is high and you buy when it is low, without trying to pick the right moment. The goal is not to hit the top or the bottom of the market. It is to stay disciplined for a long time, without relying on a hunch that makes you stop or speed up. This is the part that sounds unexciting, and it is also the part most people give up on. Think of it as the monthly savings you put aside, except that instead of leaving it sitting still, you put it into an asset.

Let's look at an illustrative example, with made-up, round prices, just to understand the mechanics. Imagine you buy one hundred euros a month, for three months, in a hypothetical asset. In the first month it costs fifty euros, so you buy two units. In the second month the price drops to twenty-five, and with the same one hundred euros you buy four units. In the third month it goes back up to fifty, and you buy two more. In total you have eight units and you have spent three hundred euros. Your average price per unit is thirty-seven euros and fifty cents. Notice that this figure is below the simple average of the three prices, because you bought more when it was cheap.

Now the comparison. If you had invested the full three hundred euros in the first month, at fifty euros, you would have six units. At the end of the example, with the price back at fifty, the eight DCA units are worth four hundred euros, and the six lump-sum units are worth three hundred. But look at the middle of the path. After the second month, with the price at twenty-five, DCA has six units, worth one hundred and fifty euros on two hundred invested. The lump sum also has six units and is worth one hundred and fifty, but on three hundred invested. In percentage terms, the lump sum is down fifty percent and DCA is down twenty-five percent.

Now notice what this example does not say. It does not say that DCA is always better. If the price had risen in the first month and never come back down, the lump sum would have earned much more. DCA trades part of that opportunity for less stress and less risk of buying everything at the top. It is not a guarantee of profit, and an asset can fall for years without recovering. So the amount you use in each purchase has to be an amount you can afford to lose, without it affecting your income, your bills or your sleep.

DCA works like a habit. The biggest enemy is not the market. It is your hand pressing the button when the price falls and fear kicks in, or when it rises and greed kicks in. So write the rules down before you start. How much you buy, on which day, on which platform, and what you do if one month you are short of money. Then stick to them. A rule written on a sheet of paper is worth more than ten decisions made in a hurry, in the middle of the night, with your phone in your hand. Once the rule is written, the emotion of the day no longer runs the show.

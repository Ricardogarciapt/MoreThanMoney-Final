You can have the best strategy in the world and still end up with no account. A run of big, bad trades is all it takes. The defence is not to be right more often. It is to lose a little at a time.

This is the most tedious module of level three and, for many people, the most important. Before we talk numbers, the usual warning. Educational content. The risk of loss is real, and it can be total. This lesson does not promise you will win. It promises you something more modest and more useful: that one bad trade will not take away what it took you years to build. That is the only promise a course on risk can honestly make. And even that depends on you, on following the rule every day, with no exceptions that seem reasonable at the time.

The rule we are going to use is this: you risk at most one per cent of the balance on each trade. One per cent, not whatever you think the signal deserves. If you have €1,000 in your simulated account, the maximum risk of a trade is €10. And be careful: risk is not the amount invested. It is how much you lose if the stop is hit. They are different things, and confusing the two is the origin of many accounts that disappear. Notice that the gold slice is small on purpose. A small slice does not bring you down. Illustrative example, with a simulated account, as always.

Look at these two cases, illustrative examples. In the first, you invest five hundred euros, but the stop is at two per cent. If it is hit, you lose ten euros. In the second, you invest only two hundred, but the stop is at fifteen per cent, and you lose thirty. Anyone who only looks at the amount invested thinks the first is the big risk. They are wrong. The number that controls the account is the loss at the stop. So the right question is not how much do I invest?, it is how much do I lose if the stop is hit?. That question protects the balance, and it is the one we will train throughout the module.

Write the formula at the top of your notebook: balance times one per cent. With a thousand simulated euros, the loss ceiling of each trade is ten euros. That ceiling does not change because the signal looks good to you. An exciting signal does not entitle you to risk more. A weak signal may lead you to risk less, and that is also fine. What changes from trade to trade is the size of the position, and that is next lesson. The risk stays fixed. If you ever write another number in your notebook, stop and go back to this page. The mistake is in the calculation, never in the market.

Let's be realistic. You will lose trades. That is part of the method and not a sign that the method is broken. With one per cent, a loss is a scratch. Ten losses in a row of one per cent each take roughly ten per cent off the account. It is a bad stretch, and it recovers with patience and rules. What destroys accounts is the opposite: risking ten or twenty per cent on one trade because this time is different. If you ever hear that sentence in your head, remember the formula. It does not argue. It calculates. And that is why it works.

Summary. The risk of each trade is one per cent of the balance. It is not what you invest, it is what you lose if the stop is hit. This rule protects you from streaks, because no single loss can take you out of the game. It also forces you to do something DCA has already taught you: decide the size before you feel the pressure of the market.

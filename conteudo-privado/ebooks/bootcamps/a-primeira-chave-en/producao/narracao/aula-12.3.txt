A signal is a card with four numbers. If you can read all four, you know what you are accepting before you accept it.

This is a signal. It is an illustrative example, put together for the lesson. At first glance four things matter: the entry price, the stop, the three targets, and the distance of each level in points and in percentage. The signal is a static plan. It was fixed on the candle that generated it and it does not rewrite itself when the price moves. If the price has already moved away from the entry, the plan stays exactly the same. This distinction will matter in the mission, because you will compare the written plan with what the market did afterwards.

The stop sits at a distance of one and a half ATR. ATR is the average range of recent candles. It tells you how much the asset usually moves in one hourly candle. By multiplying by one and a half, the stop is far enough that normal noise does not hit it, and close enough that a loss is the expected one. The idea is for the stop to follow the asset's real behavior, not a fixed number you chose on impulse. A stop chosen on impulse is a bet in disguise.

Let's do the maths, with an illustrative example. The entry is one hundred, and the stop sits two per cent below, that is, at ninety-eight. That is your risk distance: two per cent. Now the targets. The first, at one and a half to one, is three per cent above the entry. The second, at three to one, six per cent. The third, at six to one, twelve per cent. Notice that every number is measured against the risk, never against the absolute price. That is what lets you compare signals on assets with completely different prices.

Here is the same example in a table. Entry one hundred, stop ninety-eight, and the targets at one hundred and three, one hundred and six and one hundred and twelve. Again: illustrative example, because a number without context invites fantasy. I am not telling you the price will get there. I am telling you that, if you enter, this is what the plan asks for. Each level costs the two per cent risk and returns one and a half, three or six times that risk, if it is reached. Nothing more, and nothing less. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

To practise, copy this form into your notebook and always fill it in the same order. Entry, stop, the stop distance in percentage, and the three targets with their respective distances. If a signal does not let you fill in these fields without guessing, it is not complete enough for you yet. Don't use it. Read the card until you can fill it in from memory, without help, and only then move on to the decision. The order matters: first you read, then you decide. Pay close attention to this, because it is the basis of everything that follows. There is no shortcut here, and that is why it is worth practising.

Summary. A signal is a fixed plan with four parts. The entry is where the plan starts. The stop is where the plan admits it was wrong, measured in ATR. The targets are where the plan collects, measured in multiples of the risk. The percentage distances let you compare signals and understand the weight of each one on your balance. Write this in your notebook before you move on.

Almost everyone knows how to buy. Very few know when to sell, and even fewer know why they sold. Today we write that down before we need it.

First, the warning that will appear whenever we touch money: educational content. The risk of loss is real, and in crypto it can be total. With that said, let us go to a question that almost nobody answers seriously: when do you take profit? Many people only think about it once the price has already risen or already fallen, and then the decision is emotional. Greed on one side, fear on the other. The idea of this lesson is simple: the decision to sell has to be taken on a calm day, on paper, with rules you wrote beforehand. When the market accelerates, it is not the time to think. It is the time to carry out what you have already decided.

We call this a profit-taking ladder. Each rung is a level of gain, measured against your average purchase price. On each rung you sell a fixed part of the position, and the rest stays with you. Notice what this does: the part you sell is converted into euros, and the part you keep continues to take part if the market keeps rising. It is not a magic formula, and it does not tell you when the top arrives. It only tells you what you will do on each rung, before the rung is reached. That is what takes the drama out of the decision.

I will give you an illustrative example, just so you can see the mechanics. Imagine a position with an average price of one hundred. On the first rung, at plus fifty per cent, you sell a fifth of the position. On the second rung, at plus one hundred per cent, you sell another fifth. On the third, at plus two hundred per cent, you sell one more fifth. What is left stays with you, under its own rule. The numbers are mine, chosen for the example. They are not a forecast, not a target and not a recommendation. You choose your own rungs and your own percentages, according to your plan and your tolerance for risk. What matters is that they are written down.

Each rung has three pieces of information, and all three have to be written down. First: the rung, as a percentage against your average price. Second: the part, as a fraction of your position and not in euros, so the rule still makes sense when the position changes. Third: the date or the condition for review. For example, I review the ladder on the first Friday of every month. If there is neither a date nor a condition, the rule does not exist, it is just a wish. And there is one thing the ladder does not replace: the DCA. Purchases still happen on your day, as in module seven. Selling a part is a separate decision, with its own role.

There are three mistakes I see often, and the ladder prevents them. The first is changing the rule halfway, because the price is running and you want to see more. The second is selling everything at once out of fear, on a day of panic, and then watching the rise without your part. The third is selling nothing and calling that patience, when in fact it is a lack of plan. With the ladder written down, any of these mistakes has to go through paper first. That alone gives you a pause. And in fast markets, a pause is worth more than it seems.

Five-minute exercise, to do today. Take your notebook and write your ladder for one position in your plan. Three rungs are enough. For each one, write the rung, the part and the review date. You do not have to get it right. You have to write it down, and do it calmly, without the screen open beside you. Then keep the sheet next to the plan from module seven, so the two can be read together. Next lesson we talk about what to do when the market does not rise, but falls in a brutal way, and why that plan also has to be written down before it is needed.

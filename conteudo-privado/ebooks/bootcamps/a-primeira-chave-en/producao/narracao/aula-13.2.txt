You already know how much you can lose: ten euros. The question almost nobody asks is still missing: how much can I buy so that, if the stop is hit, I lose exactly those ten?

Let's use the module twelve example, with the entry at one hundred and the stop two per cent below, at ninety-eight. Illustrative example, as always. The simulated account has a thousand euros and the maximum risk is ten. The question is: how much should I have invested in this position so that the stop, if hit, costs exactly ten euros? It is not a number you guess. It has a formula, and the formula fits on a card. I will show it to you slowly, with the calculator in hand, so that you can redo the calculation yourself without looking at me.

The formula is this: the size of the position equals the risk in euros divided by the stop distance in percentage. The risk is ten euros. The stop distance is two per cent. Ten divided by two per cent, or by zero point zero two in the calculator, gives five hundred. Five hundred euros of position. If the price falls two per cent, you lose two per cent of five hundred, which is ten euros. Exactly your risk, not a cent more. Each piece has a role: the risk sets the ceiling, the stop sets the distance, and the size is what fits between the two.

Notice what happens when you change only the stop distance, keeping the risk at ten euros. With the stop at two per cent, the position is five hundred euros. With the stop at four per cent, it drops to two hundred and fifty. With the stop at five per cent, it is two hundred. The risk is always ten. A wider stop forces a smaller position. A tighter stop lets you buy more. Illustrative example, but the lesson is firm: never choose the size before you know where the stop is. The stop decides the position, not the other way round.

Write the procedure in four steps, always in the same order. One: the account balance. Two: the risk, one per cent of that balance. Three: the stop distance in percentage, taken from the signal plan. Four: you divide two by three. That result is the size. If you don't have the stop distance, you don't have a signal, and no size can be calculated. Don't guess the distance or round it to whatever suits you. It comes from the plan written before you entered, and that is where it should stay.

Second example, also illustrative. The stop is now at three per cent. Ten divided by zero point zero three gives three hundred and thirty-three. You don't round up. Rounding up is increasing the risk without meaning to, and an account that grows without you noticing is exactly what we want to avoid. You round down, to three hundred and thirty, without hesitating. Notice that the wider stop gave a smaller position, as we saw a moment ago. The rule always adds up, and that is what makes it reliable. When it doesn't, the mistake is in the calculation.

Summary. Stop first, size after. The formula is the risk in euros divided by the stop distance in percentage, and you always round down. There is no opinion in this calculation. There is a distance, a risk and a result. If one day you feel the size should be bigger because the signal is good, remember this lesson: conviction is not part of the formula.

Sometimes a single swapped letter in a website address is enough to make you lose your entire wallet. Let's see how that happens.

Let's talk about phishing. It's a copy. Scammers build a site that looks identical to the original, with the same logo, the same colors and the same buttons. Often only one letter in the address changes, or they use a lookalike domain with one extra little dash. When you get there, you type in your password or connect your wallet, and whoever set up the site keeps whatever you gave them. The site looks right, but the address is different. And the difference is in the address, which is what saves you. These sites are built in minutes and tend to last only briefly, because they're reported quickly. So don't trust the appearance, or how fast the page loads.

There's an extra caution here. Sponsored ads at the top of search engines can also lead to fake sites. Don't trust a result just because it appears first. Look for the official site, check the address and, if you can, save it in your browser bookmarks. Notice that the genuine site rarely asks you for anything urgent on its homepage. If a page pressures you to enter details, be suspicious of the page itself.

Airdrops are free distributions of tokens. They do exist, but they're rare, and they almost never arrive by private message from a stranger. When someone offers you free tokens and asks you to connect your wallet to a site, you're giving a permission. And that permission can allow someone to move your funds. The red counter is the same old pressure, dressed up as a game. If something is free and asks you to connect your wallet, be suspicious first and research afterwards. If someone asks you to share your screen while you claim the prize, it's even more serious, because they can see everything you do.

Here's the technical detail most people never read. When you use some decentralized applications, the wallet asks for an approval. It's an authorization for a contract to move certain tokens you hold. Often, the approval comes with an unlimited amount already selected by default. If you approve a malicious contract with unlimited permission, it can withdraw those tokens whenever it likes, without asking you again. So always read what you're authorizing. And revoke the approvals you no longer use, with the official permission-revocation tools.

Let me tell you an illustrative case. Gonçalo, twenty-six, a student in Braga, received a link to claim an airdrop. The page was attractive, it had a countdown, and even a list of winners that kept updating. He connected his wallet, approved something he didn't understand and went to bed. Days later, he noticed tokens were missing. Nobody had asked him for his seed phrase, but he had given permission to whoever wanted it. The mistake wasn't technical. It was haste, and haste is exactly what these sites are designed to provoke.

Before any connection or approval, ask yourself three questions. Who is asking? If you don't have a clear answer, that's a warning sign. Why is this happening now? If the justification is urgency, or a prize that expires today, be suspicious. And what am I authorizing, exactly? If you can't answer that precisely, don't sign. Every signature is a decision, not a courtesy click. You can hold on to a simple rule, and it's worth repeating: if you don't understand it, don't sign it. No prize is worth losing your wallet. And if you've already signed something suspicious, revoke that permission as soon as you can and check your balances. That doesn't mean everything is lost, but it does mean you need to act now, not tomorrow.

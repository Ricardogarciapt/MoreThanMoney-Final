The worst decision of a market day is almost never the first. It is the fourth, made to win back the third.

We have reached the last lesson of the module, and the part that decides whether the others are worth anything. Stopping rules. These are the rules that say when you stop, before you start arguing with yourself. There can be three: a maximum number of consecutive losses, a maximum loss amount for the day, and a time after which you do not enter. Write them with the numbers you choose. There is no magic number, not even mine. There is your number, written beforehand and kept. And if the number is hit, the day ends there, even if the chart is doing exactly what you wanted.

Now psychology, without beating around the bush. There is a classic spiral. You lose a trade and feel the urge to win it back. You increase the size of the next one, to recover faster. You lose again, because the bigger position amplifies the loss, and you enter a third to plug the hole. Each step seems reasonable at the time. Together they are a disaster. Break this spiral with a simple rule: never increase the size after a loss. The size is the one in the plan, always, the one you wrote before any emotion showed up. Notice that the spiral does not need greed. The urge to plug a hole is enough.

And when you feel emotion taking over, use the pause. If you have hit a stopping rule, or if you feel you can't think clearly, close the WebTrader and don't come back that day. If you really want to look at a market again, wait twenty-four hours and come back with the journal in front of you. The pause is not weakness. It is the cheapest rule there is, and the one that saves the most money for whoever uses it. If someone tells you that you have to decide now, because the opportunity won't come back, you already know what that is. Close the conversation, breathe, and go back to your plan.

Illustrative case: Tiago, forty, had no stopping rule. One morning, he lost two trades in a row and decided he would recover before lunch. It was not the first time he had thought that way, but it was the first time the journal was in front of him. He wrote, without meaning to: I want to win it back. That sentence gave everything away. He closed the screen. At the Friday review, he realised the losses that morning had the same origin: haste. Illustrative example, of course, because what matters is the pattern, not the value of each trade. Think about this during your next week, before you need it.

The level three mission, which is optional, and which is the most demanding in this course: thirty trades in a simulated account, with a complete journal, before you think about a real account. Thirty. Not thirty days of luck. Thirty trades with the rules written beforehand, with the size from the plan, and with the weekly review done. Each trade with its journal line filled in, including the rule column. If at the end you have not followed the rules consistently, the mission is not complete, and you start again more calmly. Nobody needs level three to be an investor with DCA. And think about a real account only after this, and only with money you are prepared to lose completely.

If someone guarantees that your money will double in a few days, the first thing you should feel is distrust, not excitement.

This is the oldest promise in the world in new clothes. You send crypto to an address, or you join a group, and they tell you you'll get double back in a short time. Sometimes it's presented as a prize draw, sometimes as a sign-up bonus, sometimes as a rewards programme that levels up. The format changes, but the structure is almost always the same. Before anything else, ask yourself one very simple question: where does the money for that doubling come from? If there's no answer, the conversation ends there.

In a pyramid scheme, the money paid to the first participants doesn't come from any real product or activity. It comes from the people who join later. To pay those who joined early, more and more people have to keep joining. And here's the arithmetic that's usually hidden. Imagine that each person has to bring in two new people to move up a level. By the tenth level, that step alone needs one thousand and twenty-four new people. By the twentieth, it's more than a million. No market can sustain that sum for long, and whoever joins late is the one left paying.

There are signs that keep repeating. They promise a fixed, high return, with no risk explained. They create time pressure, with phrases like today only or last place. They pay bonuses for bringing in friends, rather than for using a product or a service. And when you ask to take money out, fees, deadlines or new requirements appear, as if you had to pay to get out. If you see two or more of these signs at the same time, it's not the moment to study the offer calmly. It's the moment to leave.

Let me tell you an illustrative case. Tiago, thirty-four, a refrigeration technician in Leiria, joined a group because a friend told him he had got double his money back. At first, everything seemed to work, and they even paid him a small amount to build his trust. Then they asked him for another two hundred euros to level up. When he tried to withdraw what he had, the request was under review for weeks. He realized too late that the payments he was receiving came from the money of the people who joined after him. As this is an illustrative case, the numbers belong to nobody, but the pattern repeats in many stories.

The question you should always ask is this: who pays the profit? In a real investment, the return comes from something real, such as an activity, a company or a market with genuine supply and demand. It can go up or down, and you can lose everything, but there's a visible source. If the answer is the people who join next, you're looking at a pyramid, however well packaged it is. And pay attention to the pressure to invite other people, because that's how the scheme grows. When your success depends on bringing in people, the product is the entry itself.

Notice a detail that fools a lot of people. The first payments are usually real. They're there precisely to give you confidence. The problem shows up when you want to withdraw a large amount, and that's when the door slowly closes. So never judge an offer by what you've already received. Judge it by how easily you're allowed to get out. If you can't get out easily, it isn't an investment, it's a trap with a nice-looking receipt. So what do you do when someone promises you double? You don't join, you don't send anything to test it, and you don't ask the contact who wrote to you for more details. Search for the project's name together with the word scam and read what other people have said. And before any money decision, apply the 24-hour rule, which we'll look at more closely in the checklist. An honest offer can handle that wait without any problem.

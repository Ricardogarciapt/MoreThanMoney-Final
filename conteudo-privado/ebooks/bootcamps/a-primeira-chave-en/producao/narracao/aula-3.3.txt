You press the buy button, and the price on the screen isn't the price you pay. Let's understand why.

When you place an order on a platform, you have to choose an order type. The two most common are the market order and the limit order. They sound like technical jargon, but they decide what happens to your money. Let's take them one at a time, calmly, because this is one of the points where people most often slip up on their very first purchase.

A market order tells the platform: buy now, at whatever price is available. It's fast, and it guarantees the purchase goes through. The risk is that the price can move between the moment you press the button and the moment the order is executed. In fast markets, that's called slippage, and it can be significant. You're certain to buy, but you're not certain of the price. Think of a market stall: you pay the price written on the sign. On a busy platform, the price can change between when you press the button and when the order arrives. It's the same idea, just at a different speed.

A limit order says: only buy if the price is equal to or lower than this value that I choose. You control the maximum price you'll pay. The risk is that the order never gets filled, if the price never reaches your level. You're left without a purchase, and nothing happens. It seems weak, but it's a form of protection. In practice, a limit order won't buy for you at the worst moment. It can also be only partly filled if there isn't enough liquidity at that price. Read the platform's terms on this point, because they vary from place to place.

Let's go with an illustrative example and invented numbers. Imagine an asset that's trading at one hundred euros, and you want to buy one hundred euros' worth of exposure. With a market order, you buy straight away, and the price may rise to one hundred and two in the second it takes to execute. With a limit order at ninety-eight, you only buy if the price falls to that level. If it doesn't fall, you don't buy. Neither one is the universal answer. Each one chooses a different risk.

Notice what this means. A market order trades certainty of price for certainty of execution. A limit order trades certainty of execution for certainty of price. There isn't one that's better in every situation. Anyone who says one order type is always superior to the other is oversimplifying, and in markets, oversimplifying tends to be expensive. There's one that suits what you're doing, the time you have and how much price movement bothers you. Think about time too: if an order stays open for days, the market can change completely, and the order you wrote yesterday may no longer reflect what you want today.

For someone just starting out, the practical rule is this. Use a limit order when you're not in a hurry and want to control the price. Use a market order when you need to get in now and you accept the price movement. But never use a market order in a moment of panic, because that's when the price moves the most. If the screen is red and you feel the urge to get in right now, that urge is your signal to stop and read your notes. And watch out for a common mistake: setting a limit too close to the current price, thinking it's a bargain, and then staring at an order that never fills. A limit has to be a decision, not a guess.

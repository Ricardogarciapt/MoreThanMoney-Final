Thirty minutes a week, with the journal open in front of you. Without this routine, the journal is just a notebook full of lines.

This is the routine. Thirty minutes, once a week, always on the same day and at the same time. It breaks down like this: five minutes to prepare, ten to review the week's trades, ten to audit the rules, and five to decide on one single thing to change. Notice the time. Thirty minutes is short on purpose. A review that lasts three hours turns into anxiety, and a five-minute review turns into an excuse. Thirty minutes is enough to be honest, and not so long that you want to change everything at once. Choose the day and time in your calendar, as if it were a booked meeting.

First, review the trades. You open the week's journal and read each line from top to bottom, without editing anything. You look for three things: how many trades you made, how many followed the rule, and at what moments you stepped out of the process. Don't look at the result in euros as a judge. The result is a data point, and a single data point deceives. A week of profit with three rules broken is a bad week. A week of loss with all rules followed is a well-trained week. Read with a pencil in hand and mark the lines that make you uncomfortable, without correcting them.

Then, the audit. For each rule written in your plan, you answer yes or no, with evidence taken from the journal. Was the size within what you defined? Was the stop moved? Was there any entry with no written reason? Any no is a point of attention, and every point of attention has a cause. The most frequent cause is almost never technical. It is emotional: boredom, haste, the urge to recover, fear of missing the rise. Give it a name, write it beside the line, and see whether it repeats the following week. It is not a punishment. It is a cool-headed reading, done with the same calm you would bring to reviewing any other process.

Now, the hardest step: deciding one thing. Only one. Ask yourself which mistake repeated the most, and write a concrete rule to avoid it. For example: from now on, no order goes in without a written reason. And here is the golden rule of this lesson: don't change the rules halfway through the week. Change them on review day, after you have seen them written down. Changing everything on the back of the last trade is the quickest way to never know what works and what doesn't. If the change is too big, shrink it until it fits in a single sentence.

Last step, and this one is not to be skipped: log the review itself. Date, number of trades, number of rules followed, and the change you chose. That way, three months from now, you can see whether your discipline went up or down, and not just what you feel about it. Thirty minutes a week, without fail, is worth more than a study marathon that never repeats. And if one week you can't, do the short version: ten minutes, just the audit. Something small and done beats something big and postponed. Without a log, the review did not happen.

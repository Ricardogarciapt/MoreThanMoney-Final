There's a coin that will only ever exist in 21 million units. Sounds like good news. But there's a detail: how do those units come into existence?

Bitcoin has a programmed limit: twenty-one million units, at most. That number wasn't chosen by chance, and it doesn't change by the decision of one person or one company. It is written into the protocol, and anyone who uses the network accepts that rule when they use it. It sounds simple, but it has an important consequence: the quantity of coins never grows without limit, and that is different from almost every currency you know.

This is the halving, which means cut in half. Every two hundred and ten thousand blocks, the reward is cut in half. Since blocks come out, on average, every ten minutes, this happens roughly every four years. It isn't exact, because the pace varies a little, but it's the right order of magnitude for you to see the pattern.

Let's go through the sequence, which is what matters. Fifty units per block until 2012. Then twenty-five. In 2016, twelve point five. In 2020, six point two five. In 2024, three point one two five. Notice the pattern. Each cut slows the pace at which new units are created. The more time passes, the fewer new coins come in. That's why people who see Bitcoin as a store of value use this argument: it's a supply that shrinks over time, unlike currencies that central banks can issue with no technical limit.

Now the counterpoint, because there has to be one. Less new supply doesn't guarantee a high price. The price depends on how many people want to buy, how many want to sell, news, mood, regulation, and everything else. Think of a limited edition of a book: the print run is fixed, but nobody guarantees that readers will show up. A cut in the reward is one factor among many. History shows that the effect on the price is neither automatic nor predictable, and anyone who sells you a certainty on this point is selling you a fantasy.

Let's do the sum, so you can see the number is real. If you add fifty, twenty-five, twelve point five, and so on, each period lasts two hundred and ten thousand blocks. If you multiply the sum of the rewards by that number of blocks, you get roughly twenty-one million. That's why the limit is what it is. It isn't a round number chosen to look nice; it's the result of a series that keeps shrinking. If you do the sum on your phone's calculator, you'll see the result approaches twenty-one million and never goes past it.

Illustrative example, with round, invented numbers. Imagine a cake that only has one hundred slices. In the first days, each person who helps bake gets ten slices. Then they get five. Then two and a half. The cake never goes past one hundred slices, and new slices become rarer and rarer. But this says nothing about whether the cake is good, or whether it's worth eating. Scarcity is a property, not an investment thesis.

Imagine a bet where, if the price moves one percent against you, you lose everything you put in. It sounds exaggerated. With very high leverage, in perpetuals, it can happen in seconds.

A perpetual is a derivative contract with no expiry date. Instead of buying the asset, you bet that the price will go up or down. You can go long, if you think it will rise, or short, if you think it will fall. There is often a funding rate, paid periodically between those who are long and those who are short, depending on the difference between the contract price and the spot market price. Leverage lets you open a position much larger than the money you have deposited. This is where the risk multiplies, because what you gain and what you lose is calculated on the whole position, not on your deposit. That means a small price movement can represent a huge slice of your money.

Here are the approximate numbers, leaving out fees. At two times, a drop of about fifty percent liquidates the position. At five times, about twenty percent. At ten times, about ten. At twenty times, about five. At fifty times, about two percent. And at one hundred times, about one percent. One percent. In a market like crypto, which can move one percent without much effort in a single afternoon, this is not leverage. It is a roulette wheel with your money. Notice that the higher the leverage, the shorter the path to liquidation, and there is no room for error. And be careful: fees and the maintenance margin make liquidation happen even sooner than the table suggests.

Illustrative case: Hugo, 27, a programmer in Lisbon, opened a position with fifty times leverage, because, in his own words, he only needed to be right once. The price made a small move against him, just over two percent, and the position was liquidated in minutes. He lost the whole margin, including money he had saved for other things. He did not lose because he was a bad programmer. He lost because the structure of the product amplifies every mistake, and he had no written limit before he went in. When leverage is high, every small move weighs as much as a big one.

So my recommendation is clear: do not start here. A beginner does not yet have a solid reading of the market, does not yet have tested risk rules, and does not yet know how to handle the emotion of being in a losing position. Perpetuals amplify everything, the right calls and the wrong ones, and the amplification of mistakes usually arrives first. If your first experience is a perpetual, the first lesson can be an expensive one. First learn to buy, to hold and to record. Only after that, if you want, study derivatives, always with money you are prepared to lose in full.

Even when there is a signal, a scanner or a tool pointing to an entry, the decision and the risk are still yours. A signal does not give you the position size, does not give you the loss limit, and does not tell you what to do when the price goes against you. If you enter just because an urgent notification arrived, you are delegating something you cannot delegate. Always ask yourself: if this tool fails, do I know what I will do? If the answer is no, do not enter. And if the answer is yes, still think about the position size before you go ahead.

Keep this idea: leverage does not create money. It only amplifies what already happens in the market, in both directions. If you cannot explain liquidation in your own words, you are not ready to touch it. Write the explanation on a piece of paper, without looking at your notes, and compare it afterwards.
